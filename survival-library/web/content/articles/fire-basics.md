Fire gives warmth, safe water, cooked food, light, signalling and morale. Most failed fires fail from **poor preparation**, not poor ignition.

## The fire triangle

Fire needs **heat + fuel + oxygen**. Remove any one and it dies. Most beginner mistakes: smothering it (no oxygen), or adding big wood too soon (not enough heat).

## Choose the site

- Sheltered from wind, but **not under overhanging branches**, dry grass or roots (roots and peat can smoulder underground for days and re-emerge).
- Clear a circle 3 m (10 ft) wide down to mineral soil. Ring it with stones (not river stones — trapped water can make them burst).
- On snow or wet ground, build on a platform of green logs or flat stones.
- Keep water or soil nearby to put it out.
- **Indoors:** only in a real fireplace or stove with a working chimney. Never use charcoal, gas grills or generators inside, in garages or tents. Carbon monoxide has no smell and kills sleeping people.

## Gather everything BEFORE you light

Collect at least **twice** what you think you need, sorted into piles:

| Material | Size | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| **Tinder** | Hair-thin, a double handful | Dry grass, birch bark, cedar/juniper inner bark shredded, cattail fluff, pine needles (dead, brown), wood shavings, char cloth, cotton balls with petroleum jelly, dryer lint, paper, fatwood scrapings |
| **Kindling** | Matchstick to pencil thick | Dead twigs that **snap** cleanly (if they bend, they're wet or green), split sticks, feather sticks |
| **Fuel** | Thumb thick, then wrist thick and larger | Dead standing wood, dead branches still on trees. Split larger pieces — the inside is dry |

**Dead and off the ground** is the rule. Wood lying on the ground soaks up moisture.

### Feather sticks

Carve long thin curls down a dry stick, leaving them attached at the bottom. Make a dense cluster of curls. In wet weather, split wood to reach the dry core and carve feather sticks from it.

### Fatwood

Resin-soaked pine from stumps and branch junctions of dead pines. Smells strongly of turpentine, is heavy and amber coloured. Scrapings light even when wet.

## Fire lays

- **Teepee**: tinder in the centre, kindling leaned around it in a cone. Fast and hot; good for starting and boiling water. Collapses as it burns — feed it.
- **Log cabin**: square of crossed sticks around a teepee. Steady, long burning, good coals for cooking.
- **Lean-to**: drive a stick into the ground at an angle over the tinder, lean kindling against it. Good in wind (put the stick upwind).
- **Star / Hunter's fire**: logs pointed into the centre like wheel spokes; push them in as they burn. Very fuel-efficient for overnight.
- **Long fire**: two logs side by side, fire between them. Good to sleep alongside and for supporting pots.
- **Dakota fire hole**: two holes connected by a tunnel — one for the fire, one upwind to feed air. Very little smoke, hard to see at night, efficient and wind-proof. Good for cooking. Don't dig in roots or peat.

## Lighting sequence

1. Make a loose bird's-nest of tinder (air must get through).
2. Light the tinder at the **upwind** side, low down.
3. Add the smallest kindling as soon as flames rise — gently, don't crush the flame.
4. Increase size gradually: each new piece about twice the size of the last.
5. Blow gently at the **base** of the flame, not at the top.

## Putting it out

**Drown, stir, drown, feel.** Pour water, stir the ashes, pour again, then feel with the back of your hand. If it's too hot to touch, it's too hot to leave. No water? Use soil, stirred in thoroughly.

## Signals and safety

- Three fires in a triangle = international distress signal. Green leaves on a fire make white smoke (seen against dark forest); rubber or oil makes black smoke (seen against snow or sand).
- Never leave a fire unattended in dry, windy weather — wildfires move faster than people can run uphill.
