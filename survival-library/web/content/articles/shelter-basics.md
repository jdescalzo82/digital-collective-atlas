Shelter protects you from the five ways your body loses heat:

| Heat loss | What it means | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| **Conduction** | Touching cold ground or wet clothes | Insulate underneath you — **more below than above** |
| **Convection** | Wind carrying heat away | Windbreak, closed shelter, wind-proof layer |
| **Radiation** | Heat radiating into the open sky | Roof, reflector, small space |
| **Evaporation** | Sweat and wet clothes | Stay dry, ventilate, avoid sweating |
| **Respiration** | Breathing out warm air | Cover nose/mouth in extreme cold |

## Pick the site

- **Hazards above**: dead trees and hanging branches ("widowmakers"), rockfall, snow-laden branches.
- **Hazards below**: dry riverbeds and gullies (flash floods), low ground (cold air pools and dew), ant nests.
- **Not on hilltops** (wind) and not in valley bottoms (cold, damp). Partway up a slope, in the lee of trees or rocks, is best.
- Near materials, water and firewood, but **at least 60 m from water** for sanitation and to avoid insects and floods.
- Visible for rescue if you want to be found; hidden if you need security.

## Urban shelter

- Choose **one small room** to heat, preferably interior, with few windows. Close off the rest of the house.
- Hang blankets over windows and doors; put cardboard or rugs on the floor.
- Group everyone together; share body heat at night.
- After an earthquake or explosion, check the building for cracks, leaning walls and gas smell before entering. If in doubt, camp outside.
- Vehicles are good windbreaks and dry places but are cold at night — insulate them and **never run the engine** in a closed space or with a blocked exhaust (snow).

## Four field shelters

### 1. Debris hut (no tools, very warm)

1. Prop a long ridge pole (head height plus a body length) on a stump, rock or forked stick at about hip height.
2. Lean sticks along both sides to make a narrow ribbed tent just wider than your body.
3. Pile leaves, grass, pine needles and small branches over it **at least an arm's length thick** (more in cold).
4. Stuff the inside with dry leaves too. Crawl in feet first and pull a bundle of debris into the doorway.

Works like a sleeping bag. Takes 2–3 hours to build.

### 2. Lean-to with fire reflector

A ridge pole between two trees, poles leaned on one side, covered with bark, branches or a tarp. Face the open side away from the wind, build a long fire 1 m in front, and a wall of stacked logs behind the fire to reflect heat into the shelter.

### 3. Tarp shelters

See *Tarp Shelters* — the fastest shelter if you have a sheet of plastic, tarp, shower curtain or emergency blanket.

### 4. Snow shelters

- **Snow trench**: dig a body-sized trench, roof it with branches/tarp and snow. Fast.
- **Quinzhee**: pile snow 2 m high, let it settle 2 hours, push 30 cm sticks in all over, then hollow it out until you reach the stick ends. Make a ventilation hole in the roof and a cold-sink entrance lower than the sleeping platform.
- Inside a snow shelter stays around 0 °C even at –30 °C outside. Always keep a vent open, especially with a candle or stove inside.

## Sleeping warm

- Ground insulation first: 20 cm+ of dry leaves/branches/cardboard, or a foam mat.
- Change into dry clothes before sleeping. Wear a hat.
- Eat fats or sugars before bed; drink warm water. Pee before sleeping (your body wastes heat keeping it warm).
- A hot water bottle (well-sealed, wrapped in a sock) at the groin or chest is very effective.
