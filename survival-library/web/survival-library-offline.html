Organising a Community

Community

After disasters, survival depends far more on neighbours than on gear. Most people behave generously in crises — build on that.

First meetings

  • Meet in a known, visible place at a set time each day.
  • Count people, list injuries and urgent needs (medical, children, elderly, disabled, pregnant, people with medications).
  • Skills inventory: medical, building, mechanics, electrics, farming, cooking, childcare, teaching, radio, languages, hunting/fishing, sewing, 3D printing, accounting.
  • Resource inventory: water sources, food, tools, vehicles, fuel, generators, solar panels, medicines, radios, seeds, animals.
  • Write everything down. Post it publicly.

Roles and teams

Small teams with a coordinator each: water, food & cooking, health & sanitation, shelter & repair, fuel & energy, communications, childcare & education, security & watch. Rotate tiring jobs. Everyone who can contributes; everyone eats.

Decisions

  • Agree simple rules early: how decisions are made (vote, consensus, elected coordinators), how food is shared, quiet hours, sanitation rules.
  • Keep meetings short and regular. Note decisions and post them.
  • Make leadership temporary and accountable — rotate or re-elect regularly.
  • Include women, elders, young people and minorities in decisions; groups that exclude people fracture.

Security

  • Night watch in pairs, in shifts. A whistle or bell system for alarms.
  • Know your neighbours; meet newcomers in daylight with several people present. Most strangers are people in need — and possible new members with skills.
  • Store critical supplies in more than one place.
  • Avoid displaying abundance.

Conflict

  • Address problems early, privately and calmly. Listen first.
  • Have a mediator respected by both sides. Focus on the problem, not the person.
  • Fair, visible rules and fair sharing prevent most conflict.

Mental health and morale

Stress, grief, fear, sleeplessness and anger are normal reactions to abnormal events.

  • Psychological first aid: make people safe, calm and connected. Listen without pushing them to talk. Provide practical help — water, food, a blanket, finding family. Give accurate information.
  • Routines help: regular meals, work, rest, and something to look forward to.
  • Keep children with their caregivers; give them simple jobs, play and school-like time.
  • Watch for people who withdraw, stop eating, talk about not wanting to live, or use alcohol heavily — stay with them, involve someone they trust.
  • Mark losses together. Celebrate small wins: the first clean water, the first harvest.

Knowledge is a resource

Teach skills to others — every skill held by one person is one illness away from being lost. Copy this library to phones, laptops and other hotspot devices. Write down what you learn locally and add it.

Fire Basics - Site, Tinder & Fire Lays

Fire

Fire gives warmth, safe water, cooked food, light, signalling and morale. Most failed fires fail from poor preparation, not poor ignition.

The fire triangle

Fire needs heat + fuel + oxygen. Remove any one and it dies. Most beginner mistakes: smothering it (no oxygen), or adding big wood too soon (not enough heat).

Choose the site

  • Sheltered from wind, but not under overhanging branches, dry grass or roots (roots and peat can smoulder underground for days and re-emerge).
  • Clear a circle 3 m (10 ft) wide down to mineral soil. Ring it with stones (not river stones — trapped water can make them burst).
  • On snow or wet ground, build on a platform of green logs or flat stones.
  • Keep water or soil nearby to put it out.
  • Indoors: only in a real fireplace or stove with a working chimney. Never use charcoal, gas grills or generators inside, in garages or tents. Carbon monoxide has no smell and kills sleeping people.

Gather everything BEFORE you light

Collect at least twice what you think you need, sorted into piles:

MaterialSizeExamples
TinderHair-thin, a double handfulDry grass, birch bark, cedar/juniper inner bark shredded, cattail fluff, pine needles (dead, brown), wood shavings, char cloth, cotton balls with petroleum jelly, dryer lint, paper, fatwood scrapings
KindlingMatchstick to pencil thickDead twigs that snap cleanly (if they bend, they're wet or green), split sticks, feather sticks
FuelThumb thick, then wrist thick and largerDead standing wood, dead branches still on trees. Split larger pieces — the inside is dry

Dead and off the ground is the rule. Wood lying on the ground soaks up moisture.

Feather sticks

Carve long thin curls down a dry stick, leaving them attached at the bottom. Make a dense cluster of curls. In wet weather, split wood to reach the dry core and carve feather sticks from it.

Fatwood

Resin-soaked pine from stumps and branch junctions of dead pines. Smells strongly of turpentine, is heavy and amber coloured. Scrapings light even when wet.

Fire lays

  • Teepee: tinder in the centre, kindling leaned around it in a cone. Fast and hot; good for starting and boiling water. Collapses as it burns — feed it.
  • Log cabin: square of crossed sticks around a teepee. Steady, long burning, good coals for cooking.
  • Lean-to: drive a stick into the ground at an angle over the tinder, lean kindling against it. Good in wind (put the stick upwind).
  • Star / Hunter's fire: logs pointed into the centre like wheel spokes; push them in as they burn. Very fuel-efficient for overnight.
  • Long fire: two logs side by side, fire between them. Good to sleep alongside and for supporting pots.
  • Dakota fire hole: two holes connected by a tunnel — one for the fire, one upwind to feed air. Very little smoke, hard to see at night, efficient and wind-proof. Good for cooking. Don't dig in roots or peat.

Lighting sequence

  1. Make a loose bird's-nest of tinder (air must get through).
  2. Light the tinder at the upwind side, low down.
  3. Add the smallest kindling as soon as flames rise — gently, don't crush the flame.
  4. Increase size gradually: each new piece about twice the size of the last.
  5. Blow gently at the base of the flame, not at the top.

Putting it out

Drown, stir, drown, feel. Pour water, stir the ashes, pour again, then feel with the back of your hand. If it's too hot to touch, it's too hot to leave. No water? Use soil, stirred in thoroughly.

Signals and safety

  • Three fires in a triangle = international distress signal. Green leaves on a fire make white smoke (seen against dark forest); rubber or oil makes black smoke (seen against snow or sand).
  • Never leave a fire unattended in dry, windy weather — wildfires move faster than people can run uphill.

Ways to Light a Fire

Fire

Carry three ways to make fire. Ranked here from easiest to hardest.

1. Lighters and matches

  • Even an empty disposable lighter still sparks: use the spark on very fine tinder (cotton with petroleum jelly, char cloth).
  • Make matches waterproof by dipping heads in melted candle wax. Scratch the wax off before striking.
  • Shield the flame with your body and cupped hands. Hold the match head down so the flame climbs the stick.

2. Ferrocerium rod (ferro rod)

Gives 3,000 °C sparks even when wet (dry it off first).

  1. Place the rod tip into the tinder.
  2. Hold the striker (knife spine, hacksaw blade, sharp-edged steel) at about 45° and still.
  3. Pull the rod back rather than pushing the striker forward — your tinder bundle stays intact.
  4. Scrape slowly first to build a pile of shavings, then strike hard to ignite them.

A printable handle for bare rods is in 3D Prints (Ferro Rod Handle).

3. Flint and steel with char cloth

Needs high-carbon steel (old file, hacksaw blade, purpose-made striker — not stainless) and a hard sharp rock (flint, chert, quartz, quartzite).

  1. Hold the char cloth on top of the rock, edge near the sharp edge.
  2. Strike the steel down across the rock edge. Hot steel particles land on the cloth and make a glowing spot.
  3. Fold the glowing cloth into a tinder nest and blow it into flame.

Making char cloth

Put 100% cotton cloth squares (old jeans, t-shirts) into a metal tin with a small nail hole in the lid. Put it in a fire. Smoke and flames jet from the hole. When it stops smoking (10–15 minutes), remove it and let it cool without opening (opening early lets it burn). Good char cloth is black, not brown, and doesn't crumble. Also works with punk wood, cattail heads or jute twine.

4. Lens (sun)

Magnifying glass, reading glasses (for far-sighted, they're convex), binocular/camera lens, a clear water-filled bag or bottle, even polished ice.

  • Focus the sun to the smallest, brightest dot on dark tinder or char cloth.
  • Hold steady until it glows, then blow gently.
  • Works only in strong sun, best between 10:00 and 14:00.
  • A fresnel lens (plastic page magnifier) is the best survival lens.

5. Battery and steel wool

Fine steel wool (#0000 is best) touched across both terminals of a 9V battery ignites instantly. With AA/D batteries, use one battery and press the wool between the + end and a wire to the – end. Phone/laptop battery packs can do it too but never pierce or short lithium cells — they can explode.

Keep steel wool away from batteries in your pack.

8. Sparks from a bow drill set, flint, or a hand-cranked generator

See Friction Fire for bow and hand drills. Any dynamo, alternator or motor spun fast enough will throw sparks across a gap or heat a fine wire red-hot — a bicycle dynamo onto steel wool lights it instantly.

6. Friction (bow drill, hand drill)

Possible with no tools at all, but hard work and needs practice and dry materials. See Friction Fire.

7. Chemical (know these, but use with care)

  • Potassium permanganate + glycerine: a teaspoon of permanganate with a few drops of glycerine ignites after 10–30 seconds. Permanganate is also a water disinfectant and antiseptic in very dilute solution.
  • Fire pistons: compress air to ignite char cloth.

Keep your fire kit

In a waterproof container: lighter, ferro rod + striker, waterproof matches, cotton balls with petroleum jelly, char cloth, a fresnel lens. Check it each season.

Friction Fire - Bow Drill & Hand Drill

Fire

Friction fire turns muscle work into an ember. It needs dry, soft, non-resinous wood and good technique. Practise when your life doesn't depend on it.

Bow drill parts

PartDescription
Hearth boardFlat board ~2 cm thick, 4–5 cm wide, 30 cm long
Spindle (drill)Straight stick 2 cm thick, 20–25 cm long, rounded point at bottom, sharp point at top
Bearing blockHard wood, stone, bone or shell with a dimple, held on top. Better: the printed Bow-Drill Bearing Block with a 608 skateboard bearing
BowA stiff, slightly curved stick about arm's length
CordParacord, bootlace, leather strip, or twisted plant fibre
Ember panLeaf or bark chip under the notch to catch the ember

Good woods (spindle and hearth from the same, or similar)

Soft, dry, dead wood that you can dent with a thumbnail: willow, cottonwood, poplar/aspen, basswood (linden), cedar (western red / eastern white), juniper, cypress, yucca stalk, sotol, elder, buckeye, clematis root, alder, sycamore. Avoid resinous pine and hardwoods like oak, maple, hickory.

Method

  1. Burn in a socket. Carve a small dip 1 cm from the edge of the hearth board. Put the spindle in it, wrap the bow cord once around the spindle (spindle on the outside of the cord, cord tight).
  2. Stance (right-handed): left foot on the hearth board next to the hole, right knee on the ground. Left hand holds the bearing block on top of the spindle, left wrist locked against the left shin to keep it steady and vertical.
  3. Saw the bow back and forth with long, full-length strokes, steady, light pressure at first. The hole will smoke and turn black.
  4. Cut the notch. Stop. Cut a V-notch from the board edge into the centre of the blackened hole — about 1/8 of the circle (like a slice of pie). This is where the hot dust collects.
  5. Put the ember pan under the notch. Drill again: steady speed, then increase pressure and speed as smoke thickens. Keep going 10–20 seconds after heavy smoke appears.
  6. Carefully lift the spindle. If the dust in the notch keeps smoking on its own, you have an ember. Fan it gently with your hand for a few seconds to let it grow.
  7. Tap the board, tip the ember onto the pan, then into a ready-made tinder nest. Fold the nest around it and blow gently, then harder as the smoke increases, until it bursts into flame.

Fault-finding

ProblemFix
Cord slips on spindleMore tension in the cord; square-off the spindle slightly; resin on cord
Spindle pops outLock the wrist on the shin, keep spindle vertical, socket deeper
Lots of smoke, no emberNotch too small or too shallow; wood damp; not enough speed at the end
Shiny, glazed holeSpindle or hearth too hard, or too much pressure without speed. Add a pinch of sand, roughen with a knife
Dust is light brown and coarseNot enough speed/pressure, or wood too hard
Dust is dark brown/black and fineGood — keep going longer
Bearing block smokes/gets hotLubricate with soap, grease, leaf, or use the 608-bearing block

Hand drill

Spin a long straight stem (mullein, cattail, yucca, horseweed, elderberry, ~60 cm) between your palms into a soft hearth (cedar, cottonwood root, yucca). Move hands down the stem quickly, then jump back to the top without losing pressure. Much harder than a bow drill; works best in dry climates.

Tinder for embers

Embers need very fine, very dry tinder: shredded cedar bark, dry grass rubbed between your palms until fluffy, cattail fluff, birch bark shavings.

Fire in Wet Weather & Cooking Fires

Fire

Rain, snow and wet wood

  • Find dry material: dead branches still on standing trees (snap test), under thick conifers, inside hollow logs, the inside of split wood, under overhangs and bark.
  • Split everything. Rain wets only the outside few millimetres of dead wood.
  • Make feather sticks from split dry cores.
  • Build a platform of sticks or flat stones so the fire isn't on wet ground or snow.
  • Shelter the fire: a tarp high above (fire can melt or ignite it — keep at least 1.5 m clearance), or a reflector wall of logs.
  • Bring lots of kindling — wet fires need a bigger bed of heat before fuel catches.
  • Birch bark burns when wet (oily). Fatwood, petroleum jelly cotton and candle stubs are excellent fire starters.
  • Dry the next pieces of wood around the fire before you need them.

Cooking fires

  • Coals cook, flames burn. Let the fire burn down to a bed of glowing coals.
  • Rocks or green-log supports for pots, or a tripod and chain. A keyhole-shaped fire pit lets you rake coals into the narrow end for cooking while the round end keeps burning.
  • Boiling water uses most fuel. Put a lid on the pot. Bring water to a boil only as long as needed for safety (1 minute).
  • Retained-heat cooking ("hay box"): bring rice, beans or stew to a boil for a few minutes, then put the pot in a box insulated with blankets, hay or clothes for 2–4 hours. It finishes cooking with no fuel. Saves up to 70% of fuel.
  • Rocket stoves: an L-shaped insulated chimney (from bricks, cans, clay) burns sticks very efficiently with little smoke.

Carbon monoxide (CO) — the silent killer

CO comes from anything burning: fires, charcoal, gas, kerosene, generators, car engines.

  • Never run generators, grills, charcoal, camp stoves or heaters inside homes, garages, tents or vehicles.
  • Generators: at least 6 m (20 ft) from windows and doors, exhaust pointed away.
  • Symptoms: headache, dizziness, nausea, confusion, drowsiness — often several people at once, and pets.
  • If suspected: get everyone into fresh air immediately. Unconscious people need rescue breathing/CPR (see First Aid Priorities).

First Aid Priorities (Primary Survey & CPR)

First Aid

This is first aid for when professional help is unavailable or delayed. If you can reach emergency services, do so first — try the emergency number even when phones seem down; text messages sometimes get through when calls do not.

D-R-S-A-B-C — in this order

  1. Danger — Is it safe for you? Fire, traffic, electricity, collapse, gas, violence. Don't become a second casualty.
  2. Response — Talk loudly, tap shoulders. "Can you hear me? Open your eyes."
  3. Send for help — shout, send someone, use radio/phone.
  4. Airway — Tilt the head back, lift the chin. Remove visible obstructions from the mouth (don't blindly sweep).
  5. Breathing — Look, listen, feel for 10 seconds. Occasional gasps are not normal breathing.
  6. Circulation / Catastrophic bleeding — Look for heavy bleeding and stop it immediately (see Bleeding Control). Massive bleeding is treated even before airway.

Unresponsive but breathing → Recovery position

  1. Kneel beside them. Place the near arm at a right angle.
  2. Bring the far arm across the chest, back of the hand against their near cheek.
  3. Bend the far knee up, then pull on that knee to roll them toward you onto their side.
  4. Tilt the head back so the airway stays open and fluids can drain.
  5. Check breathing continuously. Turn to the other side every 30 minutes.

If you suspect a neck/spine injury and they are breathing and their airway is clear, keep them still. Airway takes priority over spine.

Not breathing normally → CPR

Adult

  1. Lay them on their back on a firm surface.
  2. Heel of one hand in the centre of the chest (lower half of breastbone), other hand on top, arms straight.
  3. Push 5–6 cm deep, 100–120 times per minute. Let the chest fully rise between pushes. (The Toolkit has a CPR metronome; "Stayin' Alive" has the right rhythm.)
  4. If trained and willing: after 30 compressions, give 2 rescue breaths (tilt head, pinch nose, seal mouth, breathe for 1 second each so the chest rises). Otherwise, do continuous compressions.
  5. Swap rescuers every 2 minutes. Continue until they breathe normally, help takes over, you are exhausted, or it becomes unsafe.

Child (1 year to puberty)

Give 5 rescue breaths first. Then 30 compressions with one or two hands, one-third of chest depth (~5 cm), then 2 breaths. Repeat.

Infant (under 1 year)

Head in neutral position (not tilted far back). 5 breaths covering mouth and nose with your mouth. Compress with two fingers in the centre of the chest, one-third depth (~4 cm), 30:2.

Drowning victims

Start with 5 rescue breaths — the problem is lack of oxygen.

Realistic expectations

Without a defibrillator and hospital, CPR rarely restarts a heart that has stopped from a heart attack. It works better after drowning, electrocution, lightning, and in children. If someone has been dead for a long time (cold, stiff, obvious fatal injuries), CPR will not help — and it's OK not to start.

Choking (conscious adult or child)

  1. "Are you choking?" If they can cough, encourage coughing.
  2. If they can't cough, speak or breathe: 5 back blows between the shoulder blades with the heel of your hand, leaning them forward.
  3. Then 5 abdominal thrusts: stand behind, fist above the belly button, pull sharply in and up.
  4. Alternate 5 and 5. If they become unconscious, start CPR.

Infants: 5 back blows face-down along your forearm, then 5 chest thrusts with two fingers. Never abdominal thrusts on infants.

Secondary survey (once life threats are handled)

Head to toe: ask what happened, where it hurts. Feel gently along head, neck, chest, abdomen, pelvis, each limb. Look for bleeding, swelling, deformity, wounds. Ask about allergies, medications, medical history, last meal. Write down what you find and the time.

Shock

Pale, cold, clammy skin, fast weak pulse, fast breathing, thirst, confusion — from blood loss, burns, severe infection, dehydration.

  • Treat the cause (stop bleeding).
  • Lay them flat, keep them warm (blanket under and over), reassure them.
  • Raise legs only if no leg, hip or spine injury.
  • Small sips of water only if fully alert and no abdominal injury and help is far away.

Bleeding Control

First Aid

Someone can bleed to death in 3–5 minutes. Act immediately. Protect yourself with gloves or a plastic bag over your hands if possible — but don't delay.

Is it life-threatening?

  • Blood spurting or pumping
  • Blood pooling on the ground or soaking through clothes quickly
  • A limb has been amputated
  • The person is confused, pale, losing consciousness

1. Direct pressure (first, for all wounds)

  1. Expose the wound — cut or tear away clothing.
  2. Press hard directly on the bleeding with a cloth, dressing or your gloved hand. Use both hands, straight arms, body weight.
  3. Keep pressing for at least 10 minutes without peeking.
  4. If blood soaks through, put more cloth on top and press harder. Don't remove the first layer.
  5. Then bandage firmly over the dressing.

2. Wound packing (deep wounds: neck, armpit, groin, deep thigh)

  1. Push clean cloth (gauze, t-shirt strips) into the wound, directly onto the bleeding source.
  2. Keep stuffing until the wound is packed tight — it may take a lot.
  3. Hold firm pressure on top for at least 3 minutes (10 minutes with plain cloth).
  4. Bandage tightly over it.

3. Tourniquet (arms and legs only)

Use when direct pressure doesn't stop limb bleeding, the limb is amputated, or you can't hold pressure (many casualties, dark, danger).

Commercial tourniquet

  1. Place it 5–8 cm (2–3 in) above the wound — not on a joint. If you can't see the wound, place it "high and tight" at the top of the limb.
  2. Pull the strap as tight as possible.
  3. Twist the windlass rod until the bleeding stops and you can't feel a pulse below it. It will hurt — that's normal.
  4. Lock the windlass. Write the time on the tourniquet or the person's forehead ("TK 14:35").
  5. If bleeding continues, add a second tourniquet just above the first.

Improvised tourniquet

A strip of cloth at least 4 cm (1.5 in) wide (triangular bandage, folded scarf, belt-width cloth) — not rope, wire or string, which cut tissue and don't compress arteries.

  1. Wrap twice around the limb above the wound and tie a half knot.
  2. Lay a strong stick (windlass) on the knot, tie a full knot over it.
  3. Twist the stick until bleeding stops.
  4. Tie the stick in place with another strip.
  5. Note the time.

Tourniquet rules

  • Don't loosen it to "let blood flow" — this can cause re-bleeding and death.
  • A properly applied tourniquet can safely stay on for about 2 hours; limbs often survive much longer. Saving the life comes first.
  • If evacuation will take many hours and the bleeding was from a smaller wound, a trained person may try converting to pressure dressing / packing — loosen slowly, watch the wound, re-tighten immediately if bleeding restarts.

Special cases

  • Nosebleed: sit up, lean forward, pinch the soft part of the nose for 10–15 minutes. Don't tilt the head back.
  • Object stuck in the wound: don't pull it out. Pad around it and bandage to hold it still.
  • Chest wound with air sucking/bubbling: cover with plastic or a dressing taped on three sides, or leave open with a non-sticky dressing. Watch for worsening breathlessness — if it worsens after sealing, lift the dressing.
  • Internal bleeding (after blunt trauma: bruising on abdomen, swelling, shock signs): keep warm, lay flat, evacuate urgently.

After bleeding stops

Treat for shock: lay flat, keep warm, reassure. Give fluids only if alert and help is many hours away.

Wounds, Burns, Sprains & Fractures

First Aid

Cleaning wounds — the most important step

Infection is the main killer of wounded people when hospitals are unavailable.

  1. Wash your hands. Stop the bleeding first.
  2. Irrigate: flush the wound with lots of clean (boiled-and-cooled or drinkable) water. Use a clean plastic bag or bottle with a pin hole to squirt water at pressure. At least 1 litre for an average cut.
  3. Remove dirt and debris with clean (boiled) tweezers.
  4. Cover with a clean, non-stick dressing. Change daily or when wet or dirty.
  5. Don't close dirty, deep, or old (over 6–8 hours) wounds, or animal/human bites. Pack them lightly open with clean damp gauze and let them heal from the inside out. Closing in infection can cause abscesses and gangrene.
  6. Clean, small, fresh cuts can be closed with tape strips (butterfly closures) after cleaning.

Honey (pure) is a traditional and effective wound dressing. Avoid putting strong antiseptics (iodine, alcohol, peroxide) inside wounds repeatedly — they damage tissue; use them on surrounding skin.

Signs of infection

Increasing pain, redness, swelling, heat, pus, red streaks spreading up the limb, fever. Spreading redness or red streaks, fever and chills mean a serious infection: open and drain the wound if it was closed, soak in warm salty water, rest and raise the limb, and seek antibiotics or a medic urgently.

Tetanus

Deep, dirty puncture wounds (nails, soil, rust) can cause tetanus. Clean them thoroughly. Anyone without a vaccine in the last 10 years is at risk.

Burns

  1. Cool the burn under cool (not ice-cold) running or clean water for 20 minutes. Even up to 3 hours after the burn it still helps.
  2. Remove rings, watches and tight clothing before swelling starts. Don't pull off clothing stuck to the burn.
  3. Cover loosely with cling film / plastic wrap or a clean non-fluffy cloth. Plastic bags work for hands.
  4. Don't pop blisters, don't apply butter, oil, toothpaste or ice.
  5. Keep the person warm (cooling a large burn can cause hypothermia).
  6. Give fluids by mouth for large burns if they are alert: oral rehydration solution (see Illness & Dehydration).

Serious: burns larger than the person's hand, on the face, hands, feet, groin, or joints, deep (white, leathery, painless) burns, electrical and chemical burns, and burns with smoke inhalation (soot around mouth, hoarse voice, coughing) — airway can swell shut within hours.

Chemical burns: brush off dry chemicals, then flush with lots of water for 20+ minutes.

Sprains — R.I.C.E.

Rest, Ice (or cold water, 20 min on, wrapped), Compression (firm bandage, not cutting off circulation), Elevation above the heart. After 48–72 hours, gentle movement.

Fractures (broken bones)

Signs: pain, swelling, deformity, can't use the limb, grating feeling, bone visible.

Splinting

  1. Control bleeding. Cover open wounds over the break with a clean dressing.
  2. Check circulation below the injury: colour, warmth, pulse, feeling.
  3. Splint "as it lies" unless there's no pulse below — in which case, gently pull in line along the bone's length to straighten it, once, to restore circulation.
  4. The splint must immobilize the joint above and below the break.
  5. Splints: sticks, boards, rolled newspapers/magazines, sleeping pad, tent poles, a SAM splint. Pad them well.
  6. Tie with wide strips — not over the break. Snug, not tight.
  7. Recheck circulation after splinting and every hour. Loosen if fingers/toes go pale, blue, cold or numb.
  • Arm: splint then sling (triangle of cloth or the shirt tail pinned up).
  • Leg: splint to the other leg ("buddy splint") with padding between, or long splints both sides.
  • Fingers/toes: tape to the neighbouring finger/toe with padding between.
  • Collarbone: sling.
  • Ribs: don't bind tightly; encourage deep breaths every hour to prevent pneumonia.
  • Suspected spine: keep the head and neck still. Move only if necessary, as a unit ("log roll") with several people.
  • Pelvis or thigh: dangerous — major blood loss. Bind a sheet tightly around the hips (at the level of the hip bones), keep the knees and ankles tied together, treat for shock, evacuate.

Dislocations

Shoulder dislocations can sometimes be put back by trained people; untrained attempts can damage nerves and blood vessels. Splint in the most comfortable position and seek help. Finger dislocations often go back with a gentle straight pull.

Bites and stings

  • Snake bite: keep calm and still, remove rings, splint the limb at heart level, carry the person out if possible. No cutting, sucking, tourniquets or ice. For Australian elapids and many others, a firm pressure bandage over the whole limb is recommended locally — know your region.
  • Bee sting: scrape out the stinger. Swelling of face/throat or trouble breathing = anaphylaxis: use an adrenaline auto-injector (EpiPen) if available, lay flat (or sit up if breathing is hard).
  • Ticks: grip close to the skin with fine tweezers and pull straight out steadily. Watch for an expanding red rash or fever in the following weeks.
  • Animal bites: wash vigorously with soap and running water for 15 minutes. Rabies risk — seek vaccine if any chance.

Hypothermia, Frostbite & Heat Illness

First Aid

Hypothermia

Core body temperature below 35 °C (95 °F). Can happen at +10 °C when wet and windy.

StageSigns
MildShivering, cold pale skin, clumsy hands, the "umbles" (stumbles, mumbles, fumbles, grumbles)
ModerateViolent then decreasing shivering, confusion, drowsiness, slurred speech, poor judgement
SevereShivering stops, unconsciousness, very slow breathing and pulse — may appear dead

Treatment

  1. Get them out of wind and wet. Insulate from the ground.
  2. Handle gently — rough movement of severely cold people can trigger heart rhythm problems. Keep them lying flat.
  3. Replace wet clothes with dry ones (cut them off if needed), or wrap over wet clothes in a vapour barrier (plastic sheet / bag) then insulation.
  4. Hypothermia wrap: sleeping bags/blankets, plastic on the outside, hat, everything wrapped like a burrito.
  5. Heat packs or hot water bottles (wrapped) on the chest, armpits, and back — not on hands and feet first.
  6. If conscious and able to swallow: warm sweet drinks, high-energy food.
  7. Skin-to-skin contact with a warm person inside a sleeping bag works.
  8. Severe: check breathing for a full minute (it may be very slow). If none, start CPR. "Nobody is dead until they are warm and dead" — keep going if possible.

No alcohol, no rubbing limbs, no hot baths for moderate/severe cases.

Frostbite

Numb, white or grey-yellow, waxy, hard skin — fingers, toes, nose, ears, cheeks.

  • Frostnip (surface only, skin still soft): warm with body heat — hands in armpits, feet against a companion's belly.
  • Frostbite: rewarm only if there's no chance of refreezing (refreezing does far more damage). Rewarm in water at 37–39 °C (comfortably hot to your elbow) for 20–30 minutes. Very painful — give painkillers.
  • Don't rub with snow or anything. Don't walk on thawed feet if you can avoid it. Don't pop blisters. Pad between toes/fingers, protect from cold.

Trench foot (immersion foot)

From feet being wet and cold (not freezing) for many hours: swollen, numb, blotchy, then painful. Prevent: dry feet and change socks daily, air feet at night. Treat: clean, dry, warm gradually, raise.

Heat exhaustion

Heavy sweating, pale clammy skin, weakness, dizziness, headache, nausea, cramps. Temperature normal to mildly raised. Still thinking clearly.

  • Rest in shade, lie down with legs raised.
  • Loosen clothes, wet skin, fan.
  • Drink oral rehydration solution or water with a pinch of salt — small frequent sips.
  • Should improve within 30 minutes. If not, treat as heatstroke.

Heatstroke — EMERGENCY

Temperature over 40 °C (104 °F) with confusion, strange behaviour, seizures or collapse. Skin hot — may be dry or still sweating.

Cool immediately and aggressively — this is more important than anything else:

  1. Best: immerse up to the neck in cold water (stream, bath, tub) while supporting the head. Stop when they become clear-headed and shiver.
  2. Otherwise: soak with water and fan hard; ice or cold packs on neck, armpits and groin; wet sheets changed often.
  3. Recovery position if unconscious. Drinks only once fully alert.
  4. They need medical care even after recovering — organs may have been damaged.

Illness, Diarrhoea & Dehydration

First Aid

Oral rehydration solution (ORS) — saves lives

Diarrhoea and vomiting kill through dehydration, especially children and elderly people. ORS works far better than plain water.

Recipe (WHO):

  • 1 litre of safe (boiled and cooled) water
  • 6 level teaspoons of sugar (about 25 g)
  • ½ level teaspoon of salt (about 2.5 g)

Stir until dissolved. It should taste no saltier than tears. Too much salt is dangerous — if in doubt, add more water.

  • Give small sips often — a spoonful every 1–2 minutes for children, even if they vomit (wait 10 minutes after vomiting, then continue slowly).
  • Adults: drink 2–4 litres a day plus a cup after each loose stool. Children under 2: ¼–½ cup after each stool; older children ½–1 cup.
  • Keep breastfeeding babies. Continue normal food as soon as appetite returns — rice, porridge, bananas, soup.
  • Make fresh ORS daily.

Optional: a little mashed banana or orange juice adds potassium. Rice water (water from cooking rice) with the salt and a little sugar also works.

Danger signs — urgent help if possible

Not drinking, no urine for 8+ hours (6 for babies), sunken eyes, very sleepy or floppy, blood in stools with fever, vomiting everything, diarrhoea for more than 2 weeks. In babies: sunken soft spot on the head.

Fever

  • Not dangerous on its own up to 40 °C; it helps fight infection.
  • Keep the person cool and drinking. Paracetamol/acetaminophen or ibuprofen for comfort (follow the packet dose; no aspirin for children).
  • Danger with fever: stiff neck, purple rash that doesn't fade when you press a glass against it, severe headache, confusion, trouble breathing, seizures — these can be meningitis or sepsis. Seek help urgently.

Caring for sick people in a group

  • Isolate the sick in a separate space with good airflow. One carer if possible.
  • Wash hands with soap before and after care. Masks or cloth over the face for coughing illnesses.
  • Separate cups, plates and towels. Wash them in hot soapy water.
  • Contaminated bedding and clothes: wash in hot water, or soak in diluted bleach (1 part household bleach to 100 parts water) for 30 minutes, then wash.
  • Vomit and stool: cover with diluted bleach (1:10), wait 10 minutes, then clean up with gloves.

  • Keep a written list of everyone's medical conditions and essential medicines.

  • Never share prescription medicines without knowing the dose and allergies.

Antibiotics - What They Do and When They Matter

First Aid

Read this first. Antibiotics are prescription medicines because choosing the wrong one, or the wrong dose, causes harm: allergic reactions that kill in minutes, resistant bacteria that make the next infection untreatable, serious gut infections, and masked illness that gets worse while you think it's being treated. If a doctor, nurse, pharmacist, paramedic or midwife is reachable — even hours away by foot — that is the answer, not this page. This page explains what these drugs are so you can use what you already have, prescribed for this person, sensibly, and so you know when an infection has become an emergency.

What antibiotics cannot do

They kill bacteria. They do nothing against:

  • Colds, flu, COVID, most coughs and sore throats, most diarrhoea and vomiting — these are viruses
  • Fungal infections (thrush, athlete's foot, ringworm)
  • Parasites (malaria, worms, giardia)
  • Pain, fever or inflammation on their own

Taking antibiotics for a virus gives you every side effect and no benefit, destroys the gut bacteria that protect you, and wastes a dose that someone may desperately need later. In a long emergency, a hoarded course of antibiotics is a finite, irreplaceable resource. Most infections — including most wound infections — resolve with cleaning, rest, fluids and time.

The things that actually save lives

These matter far more than drugs, and you can do all of them:

  1. Clean wounds properly. Irrigation with lots of clean water is the single most effective anti-infection measure there is. See Wounds, Burns, Sprains & Fractures.
  2. Don't close dirty or old wounds. Closing in bacteria causes abscesses.
  3. Drain pus. An abscess will not resolve while it is full. Infected collections need to be opened and drained — this, not antibiotics, is the primary treatment.
  4. Rest and elevate an infected limb.
  5. Warm saline soaks three times a day for local infections.
  6. Handwashing, clean water and latrines prevent more infection than any drug cures. See Sanitation & Hygiene.
  7. Fluids. Most deaths from infectious diarrhoea are dehydration, treated with oral rehydration solution, not antibiotics. See Illness, Diarrhoea & Dehydration.

When an infection has become dangerous

Get medical help urgently — this is when antibiotics genuinely change outcomes:

  • Spreading redness, especially red streaks running up a limb from a wound
  • Fever with shaking chills, especially with a wound, burn or recent childbirth
  • Confusion, drowsiness, very fast breathing, fast weak pulse, low or no urine — this is sepsis, and it kills within hours
  • Infection of the face, eye socket, hand, or around the spine
  • A wound that smells foul, has grey/black tissue, or has gas crackling under the skin — possible gangrene, a surgical emergency
  • Severe abdominal pain with fever and a rigid, board-like belly
  • Stiff neck with fever and headache, or a purple rash that doesn't fade under pressure — possible meningitis
  • Any infection in a newborn, a pregnant woman, or someone with diabetes, cancer treatment, HIV or no spleen — these people get worse much faster

If you hold prescribed antibiotics

  • Use the course that was prescribed for that person, for that illness. Don't give one person's leftovers to another.
  • Finish the course as prescribed. Stopping early because you feel better selects for resistant bacteria and the infection returns harder.
  • Never split or stretch doses to "make them last". A sub-therapeutic dose is the single best way to breed resistant bacteria — it gives you the harm with none of the benefit.
  • Allergies kill. Ask before giving anything. Penicillin allergy is the most common. Signs of a severe reaction (anaphylaxis): swelling of face, lips or tongue, difficulty breathing, widespread rash, collapse — this is an emergency, use an adrenaline auto-injector if you have one and lay the person flat. A mild rash days later is usually not anaphylaxis but still means stop and get advice.
  • Pregnancy, breastfeeding and children change which drugs are safe — several common ones permanently damage children's teeth and growing cartilage. Don't guess.
  • Interactions matter: several antibiotics stop the contraceptive pill working, interact dangerously with alcohol, or with blood thinners and heart drugs.

Storage and shelf life

  • Cool, dark, dry, sealed. Heat and damp are what destroy them.
  • Most tablets and capsules stay largely potent well past the printed expiry date if stored properly — the date is a guarantee, not a cliff. Reduced potency is still a real risk and under-dosing breeds resistance.
  • Do not rely on expired or badly stored: liquid/reconstituted antibiotics (short life, days to weeks even refrigerated), tetracyclines (can degrade into a form that damages kidneys), insulin, adrenaline/EpiPens, nitroglycerine.
  • Discard anything discoloured, crumbling, smelling wrong, or any liquid that has separated.

About "fish antibiotics" and veterinary drugs

People stockpile aquarium and livestock antibiotics because they need no prescription. Understand what you're taking on: they are not manufactured to human quality standards, purity and actual content vary, dosing for humans is guesswork, and they come with none of the diagnosis that decides whether an antibiotic is even the right treatment. Most people who take them are treating a virus and getting nothing but side effects and resistance. They are a genuinely poor substitute for a clinician, and they do not make this page into a prescribing guide.

Resistance is a community problem

Every unnecessary course makes the remaining drugs less useful for everyone around you. In a prolonged crisis, with no new antibiotics coming and no laboratory to tell you what you're facing, the community's stock of working antibiotics is as precious as its clean water. The group that uses them only for genuinely serious bacterial infections — and treats everything else with cleaning, fluids, drainage, rest and good sanitation — keeps them working.

What to stock instead, long before an emergency

  • Soap, clean water, gloves, and plenty of clean dressings and gauze — these prevent far more infection than drugs cure
  • Wound irrigation gear: a large syringe or a clean squeeze bottle
  • Antiseptic for intact skin around wounds (povidone-iodine, chlorhexidine)
  • Oral rehydration salts — or the sugar and salt to make them
  • Paracetamol/acetaminophen and ibuprofen
  • Any regular prescription medicines your household depends on, with a written list of names, doses and conditions
  • A good first aid book and actual first aid training — the skill is worth more than the kit

Talk to your own doctor or pharmacist now, while the system works, about what is sensible for your household, your health conditions, and where you live. That conversation is the single most useful thing on this page.

Calorie density: fats and oils ~9 kcal/g; dry grains, beans, sugar, flour ~3.5 kcal/g; fresh vegetables 0.2–0.8 kcal/g. Oil, nuts, peanut butter, rice, flour, oats, dried beans, sugar, honey and powdered milk are the backbone of stores.

Eat in this order

  1. Perishables — fresh food, fridge contents (fridge stays cold ~4 hours unopened).
  2. Freezer — a full freezer stays frozen ~48 hours if kept closed. Food with ice crystals can be refrozen. Thawed meat must be cooked within a day.
  3. Shelf-stable — canned, dry, then long-term stores.

When in doubt, throw it out: food that smells wrong, bulging or leaking cans, deeply dented cans along the seams.

Rationing

  • Count everything and divide by people and days. Write it down.
  • Share fairly and openly — hidden food destroys trust in groups.
  • Children, pregnant women, sick people and those doing heavy work need priority.
  • Reduce exertion to reduce needs. Keep warm — cold burns calories.
  • Never ration water to save food. If water is very scarce, eat less, especially protein and salty foods.

Nutrition over months

  • Vitamin C (scurvy after 1–3 months without): fresh fruit, vegetables, sprouts, pine needle tea (not yew, Norfolk pine or ponderosa — yew is deadly), rosehips, potatoes.
  • Protein: beans + grains together give complete protein. Eggs, fish, insects, small animals.
  • Sprouting dried beans, lentils, wheat in a jar (rinse twice daily, 3–5 days) multiplies vitamins with no soil or sunlight.
  • Salt: needed for sweat losses and for preserving food. Store plenty.
  • Iodised salt prevents goitre; keep some.

Foraging Safely

Food

Only eat a wild plant if you are 100% sure what it is. Many deadly plants look like safe ones. Learn plants from local people and local guides — local knowledge beats any general rule.

The rules

  1. Identify by several features: leaves, stem, flowers, smell, habitat, season. One feature is never enough.
  2. Never eat wild mushrooms unless identified by a local expert. A few species kill with no early symptoms (death cap, destroying angel, deadly webcap).
  3. Avoid: white/yellow berries, plants with milky sap (except a few known ones like dandelion), almond smell (cyanide), three-leaved patterns (poison ivy/oak), umbrella-shaped flower clusters (carrot family has hemlock and water hemlock — among the deadliest plants in the northern hemisphere), beans and seeds in pods unless known.
  4. Don't forage next to roads, industrial sites, sprayed fields, or polluted water.
  5. Try a small amount of a newly identified food first and wait 24 hours.

About the "universal edibility test"

Survival manuals describe testing unknown plants on skin, lips, then tongue over many hours. It cannot detect many deadly poisons (which have no taste and act after a day or more), and costs more energy than it gains. Use only as a very last resort, and never with mushrooms.

Bivalves (mussels, clams, oysters) can carry deadly toxins during algal blooms ("red tides") that cooking doesn't destroy. Avoid in warm months unless locals confirm it's safe. Only gather from clean water.

Fishing & Trapping

Food

Fish are usually the most reliable wild protein. Use passive methods (lines, nets, traps) that work while you do other things.

Handline fishing

The printed Fishing Handline Spool casts and stores line with no rod.

  • Line: fishing line, thread, inner strands of paracord, dental floss.
  • Hooks: safety pins, bent wire, thorns, carved wood or bone "gorge hooks" (a sliver sharpened at both ends, tied in the middle — swallowed lengthwise, it turns sideways when pulled).
  • Bait: worms, insects, grubs, bits of fish, guts. Turn over stones and logs.
  • Where: fish shelter from current behind rocks, under overhanging banks, in deep pools, where streams enter lakes. Early morning and evening are best.
  • Night lines: tie several baited lines to a long line between branches or stakes; check at dawn.

Nets

Gill nets catch fish by the gills as they swim through. Make one with the printed Net-Making Needle and Gauge: mesh size about the width of the fish you want. Set across slow water, top floating (wood, bottles), bottom weighted (stones).

Fish traps

  • Funnel trap: a cone of sticks or bottle tops pointing inward into a basket or bottle. Fish swim in, can't find the way out. A plastic bottle with the top cut off and inverted into the body is a simple minnow trap.
  • Stone weir: in a shallow stream, build a V of stones pointing downstream into a small enclosed pool. Fish drift in and are trapped; close the entrance.
  • Tidal trap: a ring of stones on a beach at low tide fills at high tide and traps fish as the water drops.

Cleaning fish

Scale by scraping from tail to head. Slit the belly from vent to gills, remove guts, cut out the gills. Cook thoroughly. Eat or preserve within hours in warm weather. Guts make bait.

Trapping (principles)

Laws protect wildlife in normal times — follow them unless survival truly depends on it.

  • Small game (rabbits, squirrels, rats, birds) is the realistic target.
  • Set traps on active trails: look for runs through grass, droppings, feeding areas, burrow entrances.
  • Use many traps — one in ten may catch something.
  • Minimise human scent (handle with gloves, rub hands with mud/smoke).
  • Check traps at least daily — at dawn — to avoid suffering and losing catches to predators.
  • Mouse/rat snap traps baited with peanut butter or grain catch small edible rodents and birds.
  • Keep pets, children and protected species in mind when choosing where to set.

Safety with wild meat

  • Cook all meat thoroughly — wild animals carry parasites (trichinella in bears, boars, rodents; tularemia in rabbits — wear gloves when skinning).
  • Don't eat animals that seemed sick or acted strangely.
  • Liver with white spots — discard the animal.

Preserving Food Without a Fridge

Food

Drying

Removing water stops bacteria and mould.

  • Fruit and vegetables: slice 5 mm thick, lay on racks or screens in sun and breeze, cover with cloth against insects. Bring in at night. Done when leathery (fruit) or brittle (vegetables). 1–4 days.
  • Meat (jerky): lean meat only (fat goes rancid). Slice thin (3–5 mm) along the grain, salt or brine it, dry in sun and wind or over a low smoky fire until it bends and cracks but doesn't snap. Heating meat to 70 °C first (in the oven/fire or by boiling briefly) kills bacteria.
  • Fish: split open, salt heavily, dry in sun and wind.
  • Solar dryer: a black-painted box with a clear plastic lid, air inlet low, outlet high. Much faster and cleaner than open drying.

Smoking

Cold smoking (under 30 °C) flavours but doesn't cook — use it with salting. Hot smoking (above 70 °C) cooks and preserves for days to weeks. Use hardwood (not resinous pine). A teepee of poles covered with a tarp or a barrel with racks makes a smoker.

Salting

  • Dry-salt: pack meat or fish in layers of salt in a container. Roughly 1 kg salt per 3–4 kg meat. Drain the liquid. Soak in fresh water to remove salt before cooking.
  • Brine: dissolve salt until a raw egg floats in it (~20% salt).

Fermentation

  • Sauerkraut: shred cabbage, mix with 2% of its weight in salt (20 g/kg), pack tightly in a jar until liquid covers it, weigh down, leave at room temperature 1–4 weeks. Keeps for months in a cool place. Rich in vitamin C.
  • Same method works for most vegetables. If it smells rotten (not sour) or grows slime/coloured mould, discard.

Canning — botulism danger

Botulism toxin is deadly, invisible and odourless.

  • High-acid foods (fruit, tomatoes with lemon juice added, pickles in vinegar, jams) can be canned in a boiling water bath.
  • Low-acid foods (vegetables, beans, meat, fish, soups) must be canned in a pressure canner at 116 °C. Never in a water bath.
  • Discard any can or jar that is bulging, leaking, spurting, smells off, or has a broken seal.

Storing grains and dry foods

  • Keep dry, cool, dark, and sealed from insects and rodents: metal bins, food-grade buckets with tight lids, glass.
  • Freeze grain for 3 days, or seal in containers with oxygen absorbers or a candle burned inside before sealing (uses up oxygen), to kill insect eggs.
  • Bay leaves in containers deter weevils. Wood ash mixed with seed grain (not food grain) protects it.
  • Check monthly. Use first in, first out.

Nuclear & Chemical Emergencies

Hazards

Nuclear explosion

If you see a flash

Drop and cover immediately — behind anything solid, face down, hands under your body. The blast wave can arrive seconds to a minute later and shatters windows far away. Do not look at the flash.

Then: GET INSIDE, STAY INSIDE, STAY TUNED

Radioactive fallout (dust like sand or ash) starts falling within 10–20 minutes near the blast and over hours further downwind. The first hours are the most dangerous. Fallout radiation drops fast:

7–10 rule: every 7-fold increase in time, radiation falls 10-fold. After 7 hours it's 1/10 of the 1-hour level; after 2 days (49 h) 1/100; after 2 weeks 1/1000.
  • Get into the best shelter you can reach within ~10 minutes: a brick or concrete building, the centre of it, or a basement. Cars and wooden houses give little protection.
  • Stay at least 24 hours unless told otherwise or forced out (fire, collapse). Longer is better: 48–72 hours removes most of the danger.
  • Put as much mass as possible between you and the outside: walls, earth, books, water containers, furniture full of stuff. Higher floors of tall buildings (away from the roof and ground, centre of the building) also work.

Decontaminate when you enter

  1. Remove outer clothing outside or at the door — this removes ~90% of contamination. Put it in a bag, seal it, leave it away from people.
  2. Shower with soap and shampoo (no conditioner — it binds particles). No shower? Wipe exposed skin with a wet cloth, blow your nose, wipe eyelids and ears.
  3. Wash pets.

Food and water

Sealed food and water (cans, bottles, inside cupboards and fridges) is safe — wipe the outside before opening. Water from covered wells and underground pipes is generally safe. Avoid uncovered water and garden produce in the first days.

Potassium iodide (KI)

Protects only the thyroid against radioactive iodine — not against other radiation. Take only when health authorities advise, mainly for children, young adults and pregnant women. Adult dose 130 mg once a day. Iodised table salt does not work (far too little iodine). Don't drink iodine antiseptics — they are poisonous.

Radiation sickness

Nausea, vomiting, diarrhoea, fatigue — start hours after high exposure. The sooner and stronger the vomiting, the higher the dose. Supportive care: fluids (ORS), rest, hygiene to prevent infection, keep wounds clean.

Nuclear power plant accident

Follow official instructions. Typically: shelter indoors with windows closed and ventilation off, or evacuate across wind. KI if advised.

Chemical release (industrial accident, train, attack)

Signs: unusual smells, clouds or mist, many people or animals suddenly sick, choking, eyes burning.

  • Get upwind and uphill — most toxic gases are heavier than air and collect in low places.
  • If you can't escape: shelter in place — go inside, close windows and doors, turn off ventilation and air conditioning, go to an upper interior room, seal gaps with wet towels and tape.
  • Decontaminate: remove clothing (cut it off rather than pull over your head), flush skin and eyes with lots of water for 15+ minutes.
  • Don't help casualties unless you can do so without exposure.

Biological threats

Earthquakes, Floods, Fires & Storms

Hazards

Earthquake

During: Drop, Cover, Hold On. Drop to hands and knees, get under a sturdy table, hold on to it. No table: cover head and neck against an interior wall away from windows. In bed: stay there, cover head with a pillow. Outside: move away from buildings, power lines, trees. Driving: stop away from bridges and overpasses, stay in the car.

Don't run outside during shaking (falling glass and facades), and don't stand in doorways.

After:

  • Expect aftershocks. Check for injuries.
  • Check for gas (smell, hissing): open windows, turn off gas at the meter if you know how, no flames or switches, leave.
  • Check for structural damage — cracks in walls and foundations, leaning, doors that jam. If in doubt, stay out.
  • Near coasts: a strong or long earthquake (over 20 seconds) or the sea drawing back = tsunami warning. Go to high ground or inland immediately, on foot. Don't wait for an official warning. Stay away for hours — tsunamis come in several waves.
  • People trapped in rubble: call and listen, tap on pipes. Only dig if safe; shore up as you go. Crush injuries from heavy objects for hours can cause sudden collapse when released — give fluids before release if possible and get medical help.

Flood

  • Turn around, don't drown. 15 cm (6 in) of moving water can knock you off your feet; 30 cm floats a car; 60 cm sweeps away most vehicles.
  • Move to higher ground before the water arrives. Turn off electricity at the main switch if safe.
  • Avoid floodwater: sewage, chemicals, hidden debris, open manholes, live wires. Wear boots; wash thoroughly after contact; cover cuts.
  • Trapped in a building: go to the highest level (not a closed attic — you could be trapped; take something to cut through the roof). Signal from the roof.
  • Afterwards: throw away food that touched floodwater (except sealed cans, which can be cleaned and disinfected). Disinfect wells before use. Dry out buildings fast to stop mould.

Wildfire

  • Leave early. Most deaths are people fleeing too late, in cars, trapped by smoke and flames.
  • Know two routes out. Keep vehicles fuelled and facing out.
  • If trapped in a car: park away from vegetation, engine on, air vents closed, lie on the floor under a wool blanket. The car will shake and fill with smoke but is far safer than running.
  • If trapped on foot: find a cleared area, rocky ground, a road, a lake or river, a ploughed field, or an already-burned area. Lie face down, cover with soil or a wool blanket. Avoid uphill slopes, gullies and saddles (fire runs up them).
  • Smoke: N95/FFP2 masks help; wet cloth helps a little. Stay indoors with windows closed.

Hurricane, cyclone, severe storm

  • Secure or bring in loose objects. Board windows or tape won't help much — stay away from windows.
  • Shelter in an interior room on the lowest floor that won't flood.
  • The calm eye of a hurricane is not the end — the other side's winds arrive suddenly from the opposite direction.
  • Storm surge (sea flooding) is the deadliest part: evacuate low coastal areas when told.

Tornado

Go to a basement or an interior room on the lowest floor without windows (bathroom, closet). Cover yourself with a mattress or blankets, protect your head. Mobile homes and cars offer no protection — get to a sturdy building or lie flat in a ditch, covering your head.

Lightning

"When thunder roars, go indoors." If caught outside: avoid hilltops, open fields, lone trees, water and metal fences. In a group, spread out 15 m apart. If you feel your hair stand up: crouch on the balls of your feet, feet together, head down, hands over ears. Lightning victims are safe to touch — start CPR if not breathing.

Extreme cold or heat waves

See Staying Warm, Staying Cool and Hypothermia, Frostbite & Heat Illness. Check on elderly neighbours daily.

Signs of change

  • Red sky at night, shepherd's delight; red sky in the morning, shepherd's warning — generally true where weather comes from the west.

  • Wind backing (changing anticlockwise, e.g. from west to south) in the northern hemisphere often means worsening weather; veering (clockwise) usually means clearing after a front.

  • Smells are stronger, sounds carry further, and smoke hangs low before rain.
  • Morning dew or frost on grass: dry day likely. No dew on a clear night: wind or rain on the way.

  • Distant objects look unusually sharp and close: rain soon.

Thunderstorms

Count seconds between lightning flash and thunder: divide by 3 for km (5 for miles). Under 30 seconds (10 km) = within striking range. Wait 30 minutes after the last thunder before going out.

Wind chill and heat

Wind makes cold air feel much colder: at 0 °C, a 30 km/h wind feels like –6 °C. High humidity makes heat more dangerous because sweat can't evaporate.

Essential Knots

Knots & Cordage

1. Square (reef) knot — tying two ends around something

Right over left and under, then left over right and under. The two loops should lie flat and symmetrical. For bandages and bundles — not for joining ropes that will take load (it can slip).

2. Sheet bend — joining two ropes (even different sizes)

  1. Make a bight (U-shaped bend) in the thicker rope.
  2. Pass the thinner rope up through the bight, around behind both legs of the bight.
  3. Tuck it under itself (not under the bight).
  4. Pull tight. Double sheet bend: go around twice for slippery or very different ropes. This is also the netting knot.

3. Bowline — a fixed loop that won't slip or jam

"The rabbit comes out of the hole, round the tree, and back down the hole."

  1. Make a small overhand loop (the hole) in the standing part, with the working end on top.
  2. Bring the end up through the loop (rabbit out of the hole).
  3. Pass it behind the standing part (round the tree).
  4. Back down through the small loop (into the hole).
  5. Pull the standing part to tighten. Leave a long tail.

For rescue loops, tying around trees, mooring. Can loosen when not loaded — back it up with a stopper knot.

4. Clove hitch — quick attachment to a pole

Wrap around the pole, cross over the first turn, wrap again, tuck the end under the second wrap. Easy to adjust; can slip under changing load — add two half hitches. Starts and ends lashings.

5. Round turn and two half hitches — tie to a tree, post or ring

Wrap twice around the object (round turn) — this holds most of the load. Then two half hitches around the standing part. Strong, easy to untie under load.

6. Taut-line hitch — adjustable loop for guy lines

  1. Pass the rope around the stake.
  2. Make two wraps around the standing part, inside the loop (toward the stake).
  3. One more wrap around the standing part outside the first two.
  4. Slide the knot to tighten or loosen the line; friction holds it.

7. Trucker's hitch — tightening loads and ridgelines (3:1 pulley)

  1. Tie one end to an anchor.
  2. Partway along, make a fixed loop in the rope (a slipped overhand or alpine butterfly).
  3. Pass the free end around the second anchor and back up through that loop.
  4. Pull down hard — the loop acts as a pulley.
  5. Secure with two half hitches (or a slipped half hitch for quick release).

8. Figure-eight — stopper, and figure-eight loop

Stopper: make a loop, pass the end around the standing part and back through the loop — it looks like an 8. Stops a rope sliding through a hole.

Figure-eight loop (strongest common loop, used in climbing): tie a figure-eight in a doubled rope.

Bonus: Prusik knot — a grip that slides when loose and locks under load

Wrap a loop of thinner cord three times around a thicker rope, passing through itself each time. Use to climb ropes, hang gear on a ridgeline, or as a rope brake.

Lashings

  • Square lashing (poles crossing): clove hitch on the upright, wrap 3–4 times around both poles in a square, 2–3 frapping turns between the poles to tighten, finish with a clove hitch.
  • Shear lashing (two poles side by side, opened into an A-frame or bipod).
  • Tripod lashing (three poles, for cooking tripods and shelter frames).

Rope care: keep dry, out of sun, away from chemicals. Inspect for cuts and fraying. Melt synthetic rope ends or whip them with thread.

Making Rope & Cordage

Knots & Cordage

Fibre sources

  • Plants: stinging nettle (outer stem fibres, very strong), dogbane, milkweed, yucca and agave leaves, flax, hemp, cattail leaves, inner bark of willow, basswood/linden, cedar, elm.
  • Recycled: plastic bags cut into strips, old clothes, VHS/cassette tape, unravelled rope, fishing line.
  • Animal: sinew (back strap/leg tendons), rawhide strips, hair.

Preparing plant fibres

  • Nettle/dogbane/milkweed: collect dead, dry stalks in autumn (or green, then dry). Crush the stalk flat, split it, snap the woody core and peel the outer fibres away.
  • Bark: strip long sections in spring/summer, soak in water ("retting") for 1–4 weeks until the inner fibres separate.
  • Yucca/agave: pound leaves on a smooth log or soak and scrape to remove the pulp.

Reverse-wrap (two-ply) cord — no tools

  1. Take a bundle of fibres and bend it in the middle — one leg slightly longer than the other (so splices are staggered).
  2. Pinch the bend in your left hand. Twist the top strand away from you (clockwise) tightly.
  3. Then bring that strand toward you over the bottom strand (counter-clockwise).
  4. Now the bottom strand is on top: twist it away, wrap it toward you.
  5. Repeat — "twist away, wrap toward". The opposing twists lock together.
  6. To lengthen: when a strand gets short, lay new fibres alongside it and keep twisting.

A thumb-thick bundle of nettle fibre makes cord that can hold well over 50 kg.

Thicker rope

Twist three or more lengths of cord together in the opposite direction to their own twist. A rope-maker (a board with three cranked hooks) speeds this up.

Drop spindle (yarn and thread)

With the printed Drop-Spindle Whorl on a stick:

  1. Tie a leader yarn on the stick above the whorl, up and around a hook or notch at the top.
  2. Hold loose fibre (wool, cotton, plant fluff) overlapping the leader end.
  3. Spin the spindle clockwise and let it hang; let the twist run up into the fibres while you draw them out thinly with your other hand.
  4. When the spindle nears the floor, wind the new yarn onto the stick and repeat.
  5. Ply two yarns together by spinning the other way for strong thread.

Uses

Fishing line, nets, snares, lashings, bow-drill cords, sewing, bags, sandals, hammocks.

Map & Compass

Navigation

Reading a map

  • Scale: 1:25,000 means 1 cm on the map = 250 m on the ground; 1:50,000 → 1 cm = 500 m. Check the scale bar.
  • North: the top of most maps is grid north. The margin shows the angle to true and magnetic north.
  • Contour lines join points of equal height:
  • Lines close together = steep. Far apart = gentle.
  • V-shapes pointing uphill = valley/stream. V or U pointing downhill = ridge/spur.
  • Small closed rings = hilltops.
  • Saddles: a low point between two hills (an hourglass shape).
  • Grid references: read eastings (left to right, "along the corridor") first, then northings ("up the stairs").

Orienting the map

Turn the map so what's on it lines up with what you see: the river on the map runs the same direction as the real river. With a compass, turn the map until north on the map points the same way as the needle (adjusting for declination).

Taking a bearing (baseplate compass)

From the map:

  1. Lay the compass edge along the line from where you are to where you want to go, direction-of-travel arrow pointing toward the destination.
  2. Turn the dial so the orienting lines inside it are parallel with the map's north-south grid lines, "N" pointing to map north.
  3. Read the bearing at the index line. Adjust for declination (below).
  4. Hold the compass flat in front of you, turn your whole body until the red needle sits inside the orienting arrow ("red in the shed"). Walk where the travel arrow points.

Following it: pick a landmark (tree, rock) on the bearing line, walk to it, repeat. Don't stare at the compass while walking.

Declination

Magnetic north differs from true north by an angle that varies with place and year (the map margin shows it; it changes ~0.1–0.2° per year).

  • East declination: magnetic north is east of true. Map → compass: subtract. Compass → map: add.
  • West declination: map → compass: add. Compass → map: subtract.

Memory aid: "East is least, West is best" (for converting true bearings to magnetic).

Distance: pacing

Count every double step (each time your left foot lands). Measure your own pace count over a known 100 m on flat ground — typically 60–70 double paces. Uphill, in mud, at night, or with a load it rises 10–30%. Tie knots in a cord or move pebbles between pockets every 100 m. The Toolkit has a pace calculator.

Aiming off and handrails

  • Aiming off: when heading for a point on a river or road, deliberately aim to one side. When you hit the river you'll know which way to turn.
  • Handrails: follow linear features (streams, ridges, fences, power lines) to make navigation easy.
  • Catching features: a feature beyond your target that tells you you've overshot.

If you're lost

S.T.O.P. Stay put if people know where you are and will search. If you must move: go downhill to water and follow it downstream (in most regions it leads to people) — but beware cliffs and waterfalls in steep country. Mark your route (broken branches, stone piles, arrows) so you can retrace it and so searchers can follow.

Coordinates

  • Latitude: north/south of the equator, 0–90°. Longitude: east/west of Greenwich, 0–180°.
  • 1° latitude ≈ 111 km; 1 minute ≈ 1.85 km (1 nautical mile); 1 second ≈ 31 m.
  • The Maps page shows coordinates of any point you tap.

Navigating by Sun & Stars

Navigation

The sun

The sun rises roughly in the east and sets roughly in the west — exactly so only at the equinoxes (around 20 March and 22 September). In summer it rises and sets further toward the pole you're nearest to; in winter further away.

At solar noon the sun is due south (northern hemisphere, outside the tropics) or due north (southern hemisphere). Shadows are shortest at solar noon and point to the nearest pole.

Shadow-stick method (works anywhere, any time with sun)

  1. Push a straight stick upright into flat ground (about 1 m).
  2. Mark the tip of its shadow with a stone (first mark = West).
  3. Wait 15–30 minutes. Mark the new shadow tip (second mark = East).
  4. A line from the first to second mark runs west → east. Stand with the first mark on your left and the second on your right: you are facing north.

Accuracy improves the longer you wait. For best results, mark the shadow tip in the morning, then draw a circle with the shadow length as radius; in the afternoon, mark where the shadow tip touches the circle again. The line between those two points runs exactly east-west.

Watch method (analogue watch or drawn clock face)

  • Northern hemisphere: point the hour hand at the sun. Halfway between the hour hand and 12 is south.
  • Southern hemisphere: point 12 at the sun. Halfway between 12 and the hour hand is north.
  • Use standard time (subtract an hour for daylight saving time). Less accurate near the tropics and around midday.

Solar time vs clock time

Clock time is set by time zones. True solar noon depends on your longitude: it shifts 4 minutes for every degree you are from your time zone's central meridian, plus or minus up to 16 minutes through the year (the "equation of time"), plus 1 hour if daylight saving is in force. The Compass page calculates the sun's exact direction and solar noon for your location — no sensors needed.

The printed Sundial / Sun Compass shows solar time; align its shadow to the current solar time and the gnomon points to the pole.

Stars

Northern hemisphere — Polaris (North Star)

  1. Find the Big Dipper (Plough): seven bright stars shaped like a saucepan.
  2. The two stars at the end of the "pan" (away from the handle) are the Pointers.
  3. Follow them about 5 times the distance between them to a moderately bright star alone in its area — Polaris.
  4. Polaris is within 1° of true north. Its height above the horizon equals your latitude.

If the Dipper is low or hidden, find Cassiopeia (a W or M shape) on the opposite side of Polaris; the star is roughly between them.

Southern hemisphere — Southern Cross (Crux)

  1. Find the Southern Cross: four bright stars in a kite shape, plus two bright Pointer stars (Alpha and Beta Centauri) beside it.
  2. Extend the long axis of the cross about 4.5 times its length toward the horizon.
  3. Draw a line at right angles from the midpoint between the two Pointers. Where the two lines meet is the south celestial pole. Drop straight down to the horizon: that is south.

Orion (both hemispheres)

The belt (three stars in a row) rises almost exactly east and sets almost exactly west. The "sword" hanging from the belt points roughly south (northern hemisphere view).

Any star method

Fix two sticks in a line pointing at any star (not near the pole). Watch for 15 minutes:

  • Star rises → you're facing east. Star falls → facing west.
  • Star moves right → facing south (N. hemisphere). Moves left → facing north (N. hemisphere). Reverse in the southern hemisphere.

Moon

  • If the moon rises before the sun sets, the lit side is to the west. If it rises after midnight, the lit side is to the east.
  • Draw a line through the two "horns" of a crescent moon down to the horizon: it points roughly south (N. hemisphere) or north (S. hemisphere).

Natural signs (unreliable alone)

Moss, tree growth and snow melt patterns vary with local conditions. Use them only to confirm other methods. Prevailing winds shape trees and dunes consistently over large areas — learn your local wind direction.

Making an Improvised Compass

Navigation

What you need

  • A thin piece of steel: sewing needle, straightened paper clip, razor blade, wire. (Not aluminium, copper or brass.)
  • A magnet (speaker, fridge magnet, motor, hard drive, magnetic screwdriver), or silk, wool or your hair, or a battery and insulated wire.
  • A floater: leaf, cork slice, bottle cap, bit of foam or bark — or the printed Floating Needle Compass float.
  • Still water in a non-metal container (or a puddle), sheltered from wind.

Magnetise the needle

  • Magnet: stroke the needle with one end of the magnet from eye to point, one direction only, lifting the magnet away between strokes. 30–50 strokes.
  • Silk/wool/hair: stroke the needle in one direction 100+ times. Weaker, needs re-doing often.
  • Battery: coil insulated wire around the needle 20+ turns, touch the wire ends to a battery for a few seconds at a time (it heats up). Stronger with more turns.

Float it

Lay the needle on the floater on still water. It will turn slowly and settle pointing north–south.

Which end is north?

The needle can't tell you which end is which on its own. Find it once using the sun (shadow-stick method, or sunrise in the east) or the stars, then mark the north end (scratch, ink, or a thread).

Accuracy

  • Keep away from metal objects, vehicles, power lines and electronics (at least 1 m).
  • Remember magnetic declination (see Map & Compass).
  • Re-magnetise every few hours.

Hanging needle

Without water: hang the magnetised needle from a single thread tied at its balance point, inside a jar or out of the wind. It will settle north–south.

Solar

  • A panel produces roughly its rated watts × 4 Wh per day in decent sun (×1–2 in winter or cloud at high latitudes). A 100 W panel ≈ 400 Wh/day in summer.
  • Face it toward the equator (south in the northern hemisphere, north in the southern), tilted at about your latitude. Re-aim it every few hours for 30% more energy.
  • Keep it clean and unshaded — even partial shade on one cell can cut output by half.
  • Use a charge controller between the panel and a 12 V battery. USB panels can charge phones and power banks directly.

Batteries

  • Lithium (LiFePO4): best for deep cycling; lasts thousands of cycles. Don't charge below 0 °C.
  • Lead-acid (car, deep-cycle): heavy but common. Don't discharge below ~12.0 V (50%) and recharge fully soon after use. Car batteries die quickly from deep discharges.
  • Power banks and laptop batteries: handy for USB devices.
  • Scavenge: cars, UPS units, e-bikes, mobility scooters, solar garden lights (small cells + panels).
  • Never short-circuit, puncture or overheat lithium batteries. Don't charge them unattended near flammable things.

Car as a generator

A running car alternator delivers 500+ W; an inverter plugged into the 12 V socket gives ~150 W. Run the engine outdoors only (carbon monoxide). Idling uses about 1 litre of fuel per hour.

Pedal and hand power

A fit person produces 50–100 W pedalling steadily. A bicycle on a stand driving a small motor/alternator can charge phones and batteries. Hand cranks give 5–20 W.

Keeping this library alive

  • A Raspberry Pi with a 10–20 W solar panel and a 12 V battery or large power bank can run continuously.
  • Protect the device from water, heat and dust. Keep a spare SD card with the library on it, stored in a metal box.
  • Shut it down properly when you can; sudden power loss can corrupt the card (the setup includes an option to make the system read-only to prevent this).

Light

  • LED torches and headlamps are hundreds of times more efficient than candles. Solar garden lights brought inside at night give a soft light.
  • Oil lamp: a jar of vegetable oil, a cotton wick held by a wire or a tin-can lid with a hole, the wick end 1 cm above the oil. Never use petrol (gasoline).
  • Candles: never leave unattended; stand in a tin or on a plate.
  • Tallow/fat lamp: animal fat in a shell or tin with a moss or cloth wick.
  • Fire is the leading cause of death after many disasters — keep flames away from curtains and bedding, keep water or sand nearby.

Sanitation & Hygiene

Sanitation

After disasters, more people often die from disease spread by poor sanitation than from the disaster itself. Faeces must be kept away from water, food and hands.

Handwashing — the single best protection

Wash with soap and water for 20 seconds: after the toilet, before handling food, before eating, after caring for sick people, after handling animals or rubbish.

Tippy-tap (hands-free handwashing station)

  1. Make a small hole near the top of a jerrycan or bottle (opposite the handle).
  2. Hang it from a horizontal stick between two posts so it can tilt.
  3. Tie a string from the neck down to a foot-pedal stick on the ground.
  4. Step on the pedal → the can tips → water pours from the hole. Release → it swings back.
  5. Hang soap on a string. Put gravel under it so the ground doesn't turn to mud.

No soap? Wood ash or sand scrubbing is better than water alone.

Toilets

When water and sewers fail (buildings)

Don't keep flushing if sewers may be broken — sewage backs up. Use the two-bucket system:

  • Bucket 1 (urine): urine is nearly sterile; dilute 1:10 and pour on non-food plants or a soakaway away from water.
  • Bucket 2 (faeces): line with a bag, cover every use with sawdust, dry soil, ash or shredded paper. Tight lid. When full, tie the bag and bury it or store it sealed for later disposal.

Outdoors

  • Cat hole (one person, short-term): 15–20 cm deep, at least 60 m (200 ft) from water, camp and trails. Cover afterwards.
  • Trench latrine (groups): 30 cm wide, 1 m deep, as long as needed. Cover waste each time with a layer of soil. Fill in when 30 cm from the top.
  • Pit latrine (longer term): 1–2 m deep, with a slab or seat and a shelter, a lid to keep flies out, downhill and at least 30 m from wells and water sources, and above the water table.

One toilet per 20 people as a minimum. Separate for women and men where possible, lit at night, close enough to be safe.

Rubbish

  • Separate food waste (compost or bury) from other rubbish.
  • Bury, or burn outdoors downwind of camp in small amounts (not plastics if avoidable — toxic smoke).
  • Rubbish attracts rats, flies and dogs, which spread disease.

Dead animals and human remains

Bury animal carcasses deep, away from water. Human remains: handle with gloves, wrap, record identity and location for families, bury at least 1.5 m deep and away from water sources. Contrary to common fear, bodies of people killed by disasters (not infections) rarely cause epidemics — don't take risky mass action, but don't leave them in water sources.

Personal hygiene

  • Wash body daily if possible — at least face, armpits, groin and feet.
  • Brush teeth: a chewed twig, salt, baking soda, or charcoal.
  • Wash and dry clothes regularly; sun-dry bedding to kill lice and mites.
  • Menstrual hygiene: reusable cloth pads washed with soap and sun-dried.
  • Lice: comb with a fine comb daily; wash bedding in hot water.

Insects and rodents

  • Cover water and food. Keep camps clean.
  • Mosquito nets at night in malaria areas.
  • Store food off the ground in sealed containers.

Shelter Basics

Shelter & Warmth

Shelter protects you from the five ways your body loses heat:

Heat lossWhat it meansFix
ConductionTouching cold ground or wet clothesInsulate underneath you — more below than above
ConvectionWind carrying heat awayWindbreak, closed shelter, wind-proof layer
RadiationHeat radiating into the open skyRoof, reflector, small space
EvaporationSweat and wet clothesStay dry, ventilate, avoid sweating
RespirationBreathing out warm airCover nose/mouth in extreme cold

Pick the site

  • Hazards above: dead trees and hanging branches ("widowmakers"), rockfall, snow-laden branches.
  • Hazards below: dry riverbeds and gullies (flash floods), low ground (cold air pools and dew), ant nests.
  • Not on hilltops (wind) and not in valley bottoms (cold, damp). Partway up a slope, in the lee of trees or rocks, is best.
  • Near materials, water and firewood, but at least 60 m from water for sanitation and to avoid insects and floods.
  • Visible for rescue if you want to be found; hidden if you need security.

Urban shelter

  • Choose one small room to heat, preferably interior, with few windows. Close off the rest of the house.
  • Hang blankets over windows and doors; put cardboard or rugs on the floor.
  • Group everyone together; share body heat at night.
  • After an earthquake or explosion, check the building for cracks, leaning walls and gas smell before entering. If in doubt, camp outside.
  • Vehicles are good windbreaks and dry places but are cold at night — insulate them and never run the engine in a closed space or with a blocked exhaust (snow).

Four field shelters

1. Debris hut (no tools, very warm)

  1. Prop a long ridge pole (head height plus a body length) on a stump, rock or forked stick at about hip height.
  2. Lean sticks along both sides to make a narrow ribbed tent just wider than your body.
  3. Pile leaves, grass, pine needles and small branches over it at least an arm's length thick (more in cold).
  4. Stuff the inside with dry leaves too. Crawl in feet first and pull a bundle of debris into the doorway.

Works like a sleeping bag. Takes 2–3 hours to build.

2. Lean-to with fire reflector

A ridge pole between two trees, poles leaned on one side, covered with bark, branches or a tarp. Face the open side away from the wind, build a long fire 1 m in front, and a wall of stacked logs behind the fire to reflect heat into the shelter.

3. Tarp shelters

See Tarp Shelters — the fastest shelter if you have a sheet of plastic, tarp, shower curtain or emergency blanket.

4. Snow shelters

  • Snow trench: dig a body-sized trench, roof it with branches/tarp and snow. Fast.
  • Quinzhee: pile snow 2 m high, let it settle 2 hours, push 30 cm sticks in all over, then hollow it out until you reach the stick ends. Make a ventilation hole in the roof and a cold-sink entrance lower than the sleeping platform.
  • Inside a snow shelter stays around 0 °C even at –30 °C outside. Always keep a vent open, especially with a candle or stove inside.

Sleeping warm

  • Ground insulation first: 20 cm+ of dry leaves/branches/cardboard, or a foam mat.
  • Change into dry clothes before sleeping. Wear a hat.
  • Eat fats or sugars before bed; drink warm water. Pee before sleeping (your body wastes heat keeping it warm).
  • A hot water bottle (well-sealed, wrapped in a sock) at the groin or chest is very effective.

Tarp Shelters & Cordage Setups

Shelter & Warmth

A 3 × 3 m tarp, plastic sheet, or even a shower curtain can shelter two people.

Ridgeline

Tie a line tightly between two trees at about chest height:

  1. One end: bowline or round turn and two half hitches around the tree.
  2. Other end: trucker's hitch (pulls it really tight) finished with a slippery half hitch so it unties easily.

See Knots for each. The printed Guyline Tensioners let you tighten lines without re-tying.

Common pitches

  • A-frame: tarp draped over the ridgeline, corners staked out on both sides. Good in rain; lower it for wind.
  • Lean-to: one edge tied along the ridgeline, the other edge staked to the ground, back to the wind. Pairs well with a fire in front.
  • Diamond / Plow point: one corner tied high to a tree, opposite corner staked low to the ground, side corners staked out. Quick for one person, good in wind.
  • Tube tent: tarp folded around the ridgeline with the bottom folded under you. Floor and roof together.

Tips

  • Pitch the back or side to the wind and keep the tarp low in storms.
  • Tie a short drip line (a string hanging down) on the ridgeline just outside the tarp edge, so rain running along the line drips off instead of into the shelter.
  • Stakes: sharpened sticks, driven at 45° leaning away from the tarp. In sand or snow, bury a stick or bag of sand sideways ("deadman anchor") with the line tied around its middle.
  • No grommets? Put a small stone or wad of grass behind the tarp corner and tie the line around the "bubble" — no tearing.
  • Emergency (foil) blankets reflect body heat; put the shiny side toward you, or toward the fire.

Staying Warm, Staying Cool

Shelter & Warmth

Cold: the layering system

  1. Base layer against skin: wool or synthetic. Avoid cotton in cold and wet — "cotton kills" because wet cotton loses all insulation.
  2. Insulating layer: fleece, wool sweater, down or synthetic jacket. Improvised: newspapers, dry grass or leaves stuffed between two layers of clothing.
  3. Shell: wind and rain proof. Improvised: rubbish bag with holes for head and arms.

Regulate: take layers off before you sweat when working, put them on before you get cold when you stop.

Keep the extremities

  • Hat: cover the head and neck. Scarf over the face in wind.
  • Hands: mittens are warmer than gloves. Tuck hands in armpits.
  • Feet: dry socks are worth more than food. Change them daily; dry wet ones inside your clothes against your body at night. Loose boots are warmer than tight ones.

Early warning signs

Shivering, clumsy hands, stumbling, slurred speech, poor decisions ("the umbles": stumbles, mumbles, fumbles, grumbles). Stop, shelter, add layers, eat and drink something warm and sweet. See Hypothermia & Heat Illness.

Heat

  • Work early morning and evening. Rest in the shade 11:00–16:00.
  • Loose, light-coloured, long-sleeved clothing and a wide hat; keep covered.
  • Drink regularly before you are thirsty — up to 1 litre an hour during heavy work in heat. Add a pinch of salt per litre if sweating a lot for hours.
  • Wet cloth on the head and neck; sit in breeze; stay off hot ground (sit on something 30 cm above it).
  • Watch children, elderly and anyone with heart conditions closely.

Early warning signs

Heavy sweating, cramps, headache, nausea, dizziness, weakness = heat exhaustion: rest in shade, cool and drink. Confusion, hot skin, collapse = heatstroke = emergency. See Hypothermia & Heat Illness.

Signalling for Rescue

Signals & Comms

Three of anything — three fires, three whistle blasts, three shots, three flashes — is the international distress signal.

Signal mirror

Visible 15–30 km away in sun — the best daytime signal. Use a real signal mirror, a CD/DVD, a phone screen, polished metal or a mirror shard.

  1. Hold the mirror near your eye. Stretch your other arm out toward the target (aircraft, ship, distant hill) and make a V with two fingers.
  2. Catch the sun's reflection on your fingers (you'll see a bright spot).
  3. Tilt the mirror so the spot moves between your fingers onto the target. Wiggle it slightly so it flashes.

Sweep the horizon regularly even when you see nothing — pilots see flashes long before you hear them.

Ground-to-air symbols

Make them big (at least 3 m long, lines 1 m wide), with strong contrast: dark branches or rocks on snow, light stones or cloth on dark ground, trenches that cast shadows. Straight lines and right angles don't occur in nature and stand out.

Aircraft acknowledging: rocking wings (day) or flashing green lights (night).

Fire and smoke

  • Three fires in a triangle, 30 m apart, ready to light at a moment's notice.
  • White smoke (add green leaves, moss, a little water) shows against dark forest.
  • Black smoke (rubber, oil, plastic) shows against snow, sand and sky.
  • At night, flames are visible for many kilometres.

Sound

  • Whistle: three blasts, pause, repeat. Carries much further than shouting and costs little energy.
  • Banging metal on metal; car horn in patterns of three.
  • Listen after signalling: rescuers may reply with two blasts or calls.

Light (night)

  • Flashlight or phone light in SOS: three short, three long, three short (· · · — — — · · ·). The Toolkit has an SOS flasher using the phone screen.
  • Strobe or headlamp aimed at aircraft. Car headlights.
  • Chemical light sticks swung on a cord in a circle.

Leave messages

At your camp or a route junction: arrows of stones or sticks, notes in plastic bags tied high, with the date, names, condition, and direction of travel. Search teams look for these.

Bright colours

Spread bright tarps, clothing or emergency blankets on open ground or roofs. Wave bright cloth on a long pole — movement catches the eye.

The First 72 Hours

Start Here

The first three days decide most outcomes. Do things in the right order.

Stop and think: S.T.O.P.

  1. Stop. Sit down. Panic burns energy and causes injuries.
  2. Think. What is the immediate danger? What do you have?
  3. Observe. People, weather, terrain, water, daylight remaining, hazards (fire, gas, floods, damaged buildings, power lines).
  4. Plan. Decide the next one or two actions, then act.

The rule of threes (rough guide)

You can survive aboutwithout
3 minutesair, or with severe bleeding
3 hoursshelter in harsh cold or heat
3 dayswater
3 weeksfood

So: safety and first aid → shelter / warmth → water → food. Signalling and communication run alongside all of these.

Hour 0–1

  • Move away from immediate danger: fire, smoke, gas smell (no flames, no switches), floodwater, unstable buildings, downed power lines (stay 10 m away).
  • Check yourself, then others, for severe bleeding and breathing. See Bleeding Control and First Aid Priorities.
  • Account for everyone. Agree a meeting point.
  • If there is a radio, listen for official instructions. In a nuclear or chemical event: get inside, stay inside, stay tuned (see Nuclear & Chemical).

Hours 1–12

  • Shelter and warmth: get dry, get out of wind, insulate from the ground. Cold kills faster than hunger.
  • Water: fill every clean container you have now while taps still run (bathtubs, buckets, bottles). Water heaters and toilet tanks (not the bowl) hold clean-ish water.
  • Light and fire: gather fuel before dark. Never burn fuel indoors without ventilation — carbon monoxide kills silently.
  • Inventory: food, water, medicine, tools, fuel, batteries, phones, cash, documents.

Hours 12–72

  • Disinfect all drinking water (see Making Water Safe).
  • Set up sanitation away from water and food (see Sanitation & Hygiene).
  • Ration perishable food first, then fridge, then freezer, then shelf-stable.
  • Make contact: neighbours, local groups, radio nets. Share what you know; ask what's needed.
  • Make a signal visible from the air and road if you need rescue (see Signalling).
  • Sleep in shifts if security is a concern.

Daily checklist

  • [ ] Everyone accounted for, injuries checked and dressed
  • [ ] 3–4 litres of safe water per person per day (more in heat/hard work)
  • [ ] Dry, insulated sleeping place
  • [ ] Fire/fuel and light for tonight
  • [ ] Latrine in use; hands washed before food
  • [ ] Food plan for tomorrow
  • [ ] Radio checked at set times
  • [ ] Plan for tomorrow agreed

3D Printing When Supplies Are Short

Tools & Fabrication

A 3D printer can make tools, spare parts, connectors and instruments from a spool of plastic — but plastic is not steel. Know the limits.

Choosing a material

MaterialGood forAvoid for
PLAEasy to print. Instruments, guides, gauges, indoor partsHeat (softens at ~55 °C — a car in the sun), outdoor UV long-term, impact in cold (brittle)
PETGBest all-rounder: tougher, UV and water resistant, mostly food-safe resin. Tools, water gear, outdoor partsVery high heat
ABS / ASAHeat resistant to ~95 °C, ASA is UV-stable for outdoorsNeeds an enclosure; fumes — ventilate
Nylon / PCToughest: gears, hitches, wrenches, bushingsMust be dried before printing; harder to print
TPU (flexible)Gaskets, tyres, seals, grips, shoe solesRigid parts

Settings for strong parts

  • Walls (perimeters) matter more than infill: 4–6 walls for tools; 3 for light parts.
  • Infill 30–50% for tools, 100% for small high-stress parts (wrenches, hitches, pins).
  • Layer height 0.2–0.28 mm. Slightly hotter nozzle temperature (+5–10 °C) improves layer bonding.
  • Orientation: parts are weakest between layers. Lay parts so the force runs along the layers, not across them. The 3D Prints page lists the right orientation for each design.
  • Brim for tall or narrow parts.

Food and water safety

FDM prints have tiny crevices where bacteria grow. For water and food contact: PETG or "food-safe" PLA, sanitise with diluted bleach before use, replace parts often, don't use with hot food. Brass nozzles can contain lead — use stainless for food parts if you can.

Running on solar / battery power

  • A typical printer uses 50–150 W while printing (the heated bed is most of it). Disable or lower the bed heater (PLA can print on a cold bed with glue stick or painter's tape) to halve power use.
  • A 4-hour print at 80 W ≈ 320 Wh: a 200 W solar panel and a 100 Ah 12 V battery can support a few prints a day in good sun.
  • Power cuts ruin prints. Print small parts, enable "power loss recovery" if your printer supports it, and use a UPS/battery between.

Keeping a printer alive

  • Spares: nozzles (0.4 mm), PTFE tube, a hot end thermistor and heater cartridge, belts, a spare mainboard fuse, a spare power supply. Print spare printed parts (fan ducts, brackets) now.
  • Filament storage: sealed bags or boxes with desiccant (silica gel, dry rice, or oven-dried cat litter). Wet filament prints weak, stringy and bubbly. Dry it at 45–55 °C for 4–6 hours.
  • Lubricate rods with light machine oil or grease; keep dust off.

Recycling plastic

  • Failed prints and supports: grind them and melt into sheets or blocks in a sandwich press or oven (PLA ~180 °C, HDPE ~150–180 °C) for knife handles, cutting boards, tiles. Ventilate well; never burn plastic.
  • HDPE (milk jugs, ♲2) and PP (♲5) can be shredded and pressed into sheets for panels and tool handles.
  • PET bottles can be cut into tape and drawn into filament with a "bottle-to-filament" puller (designs exist) — slow, but works with any printer that handles PETG.

Design and modify

All 3D models here are OpenSCAD source files: plain text you can edit. Change the numbers at the top (size, diameter, latitude) and re-render. OpenSCAD is free and runs offline on Windows, Mac and Linux — keep a copy of the installer with your files.

Tool Care, Sharpening & Handles

Tools & Fabrication

Sharpening

A sharp tool is safer than a dull one — it needs less force and slips less.

  1. Find the angle: lay the edge on the stone and raise the spine until the bevel sits flat. The printed Angle Guides show 15°, 20° and 25°. Knives: 15–20° per side. Axes, machetes, hoes: 25–30°.
  2. Coarse stone first: stroke the blade edge-first across the stone as if slicing a thin layer off it, keeping the angle constant. Equal strokes on each side.
  3. Feel for a burr (a tiny curl of metal) along the whole opposite side of the edge — that means you've reached the edge.
  4. Fine stone: lighter strokes, alternating sides, to remove the burr.
  5. Strop on leather, cardboard or denim, edge-trailing (spine first), to polish.

Improvised stones: the unglazed ring on the bottom of a ceramic mug or plate, a smooth river stone (fine), a brick or concrete step (coarse), sandpaper on a flat board. Use water or oil on stones to carry away metal.

Axes and hoes

Use a file, pushing into the edge from the body toward the edge, holding the tool clamped or wedged. Keep a convex ("appleseed") edge on axes — stronger than a flat bevel.

Handles

  • Best woods: ash, hickory, oak, maple, hornbeam, birch. Straight grain running along the handle.
  • Cut green and let the rough-shaped handle dry several weeks indoors before final fitting — green wood shrinks and loosens.
  • Fitting an axe head: shape the top to fit tightly, drive the head on, cut a slot in the top of the handle, drive a hardwood or metal wedge in, trim flush. Soak in linseed oil.
  • Loose head in an emergency: soak the head end in water overnight to swell the wood (temporary only).
  • The printed trowel, hoe and rake heads take a 25 mm round handle — a straight sapling or broom handle.

Rust

  • Keep tools dry. Wipe with an oily rag after use. Store in a box with a little oil-soaked sawdust or sand.
  • Remove rust with steel wool, sand, or a vinegar soak (hours to a day), then oil.
  • Grease, tallow, beeswax, motor oil and vegetable oil all protect steel (vegetable oil gets sticky over time).

A basic toolkit worth protecting

Saw (bow saw or pruning saw), axe or hatchet, sharpening stones and file, hammer, pry bar, shovel, screwdrivers, adjustable wrench, pliers, multi-tool, drill (hand-powered brace if possible), knives, needle and strong thread, wire, duct tape, cord, nails, screws, glue, a tarp.

Bending & Shaping Wood

Tools & Fabrication

Curved wood is stronger and lighter than a curve cut from a straight board, because the grain follows the curve instead of being cut across it. You need curves for sled and cart runners, barrel and bucket hoops, snowshoe frames, chair and pack frames, bows, baskets, boat ribs and tool handles.

Which wood bends

Best: ash, oak (white and red), hickory, elm, beech, birch, willow, hazel, yew, cherry, walnut.

Poor: most conifers (pine, spruce, fir — they crush and split), and brittle or short-grained woods.

FactorWhy it matters
Green (fresh) woodBends far more easily than dry. Cut and bend the same week if you can
Straight grainGrain must run along the length. A run-out (grain leaving the side of the piece) is where it breaks
No knotsA knot in the bend zone will fail
Riven, not sawnSplit (riven) wood follows the grain naturally and bends much better than sawn stock

Rule of thumb: with steam, wood bends to a radius of roughly 25–30× its thickness without a strap, and much tighter with one. So 10 mm thick stock bends to a 250–300 mm radius. Thinner is easier — if a bend keeps failing, make the piece thinner and laminate two of them.

1. Soaking and heating (simplest)

Thin material — withies, willow, hazel, split cane, basket splints up to about 6 mm — often bends after a long soak in water, or a short one in hot water.

  1. Soak 2–24 hours (hot water is much faster).
  2. Bend slowly around a form while hot and wet.
  3. Tie or peg it to the form and let it dry completely — days to weeks.

2. Steam bending

The standard method for anything thicker than about 6 mm.

A steam box from scraps

  • Box: a length of plastic drain pipe, a wooden box, or a metal stove pipe. Make it only slightly bigger than the work.
  • Boiler: a metal can, kettle or pressure cooker over a fire, with a hose to the box. A fuel can with a hose soldered or clamped in works.
  • Drain hole at the low end so condensed water runs out (otherwise it floods and the steam stops).
  • Dowels through the box to rest the wood on, so steam reaches all sides.
  • Loose door or a rag plug at the end — it must not be sealed tight. A sealed steam box is a bomb. Always leave a vent.

Timing

Steam for about 1 hour per 25 mm (1 inch) of thickness. More is not better — over-steaming makes wood soggy and weak. 10 mm stock needs about 25 minutes.

Bending

You have about 30 seconds to a minute once the wood leaves the box. Everything must be ready before you open it:

  1. Form: a shape cut from thick board or made of pegs driven into a log or a board, cut to a slightly tighter curve than you want — wood springs back 5–15%.
  2. Gloves — the wood is scalding.
  3. Bend smoothly and steadily, no jerking. Start from the middle.
  4. Clamp or tie it immediately and leave it on the form until bone dry: a week for thin stock, several weeks for thick.

The compression strap (for tight bends)

Wood fails in bending because the outside stretches and tears. Wood can be squashed a lot but barely stretched at all. A strap fixes this:

  • Put a strip of thin metal (steel banding, a saw blade, a tin can cut open) along the outside of the bend.
  • Fit a solid end block at each end of the wood so the strap and wood are locked together end-to-end.
  • Now the strap carries the stretching, and the wood's outside face cannot pull apart — it compresses on the inside instead.

With a strap you can make bends several times tighter. This is how chair backs, barrel staves and snowshoe frames are made.

3. Kerf bending (no steam, works on dry boards)

Saw a series of cuts across the board, most of the way through, on the inside of the curve. The board then folds. Used for box corners, cladding curves, splints and quick frames.

  • Cut depth: about 2/3 to 3/4 of the thickness. Too deep and it snaps.
  • Spacing: closer kerfs = tighter curve. Start with cuts one board-thickness apart and test.
  • It is much weaker than a steamed bend. Glue the kerfs full of glue and sawdust, or glue and screw a backing strip over the curve, to restore some strength.

4. Lamination (strongest, most reliable)

Instead of bending one thick piece, glue several thin ones around a form. This is how skis, bows, roof beams and good tool handles are made.

  1. Split or saw several thin strips (2–5 mm) from the same board, keeping them in order.
  2. Soak or steam them if the curve is tight. Thin strips often bend cold.
  3. Spread glue on every mating face (see Glues & Joining Wood).
  4. Clamp the stack around the form — lots of clamps or tight cord wrapping, pressure everywhere along the curve.
  5. Leave until the glue is fully cured.

A laminated curve barely springs back, is stronger than the original board, and doesn't need a steam box. Its weakness is the glue, so it needs a water-resistant glue for anything that gets wet.

5. Green-wood bending and setting with fire

A traditional method with no equipment: heat the wood over coals (not flames — scorch it, don't burn it), bending gradually as it heats, and keep it clamped while it cools. Used for tool handles, snowshoes, sled runners and bows. Rub the surface with fat or oil first to stop it drying out and cracking.

Glues & Joining Wood

Tools & Fabrication

Modern glue runs out. These can all be made from what's around you — and the joints at the end of this article hold with no glue at all.

Rule for all glues: clean, dry, well-fitted surfaces and firm clamping pressure matter more than which glue you use. A thick glue line in a sloppy joint is always weak.

Hide glue (strongest traditional glue)

Made from collagen in skin, sinew, hooves and connective tissue. Very strong on wood, reversible with heat and water, used for furniture, bows, tool handles and laminations for thousands of years.

  1. Collect: rawhide scraps, skin trimmings, sinew, hooves, fish skins and bones. Scrape off all fat, hair and flesh — fat ruins glue.
  2. Soak in cold water 12–24 hours, changing the water, until soft and swollen.
  3. Simmer gently in water — about 60–70 °C, never boil. Boiling destroys the strength. A pot inside a bigger pot of water (a double boiler) makes this easy.
  4. Keep simmering for several hours, topping up water, until the liquid is syrupy and strings off the spoon.
  5. Strain through cloth.
  6. Use hot, or pour into a thin sheet to dry hard; broken into chips it stores for years. To reuse, soak chips in water until swollen, then warm.

Using it: apply hot to both faces, bring together quickly, clamp firmly. It grabs within a minute as it cools, and reaches full strength in a day. Not waterproof — a damp joint will creep and let go. That reversibility is also its advantage: steam or hot water takes a hide-glue joint apart for repair without damaging the wood.

Fish glue is the same process using fish skins, heads and swim bladders; it stays liquid at room temperature and is excellent for small work.

Pine pitch glue (fast, waterproof, brittle)

The classic hafting glue for attaching stone points, sealing seams and waterproofing containers. Sets in seconds as it cools.

Recipe by volume: about 5 parts pitch : 1 part charcoal : 1 part binder.

  1. Collect resin — hardened amber lumps on pine, spruce or fir wounds. Don't cut new wounds in healthy trees if you can avoid it.
  2. Melt gently in a tin over low coals. It is flammable — never over an open flame, and keep a lid nearby to smother it. Strain out bark through cloth or a screen.
  3. Add powdered charcoal — ground fine. This is the filler that stops it shattering.
  4. Add a binder for flexibility: rendered fat, beeswax, or dried, finely-ground animal dung. Too little and it is glass-brittle in cold; too much and it stays soft in heat.
  5. Test a drop on a cold stone: it should be hard but not shatter when struck. Adjust and re-test.

Store as blobs on a stick. To use, warm until it flows, apply, press together, let cool. Wrap the joint with sinew or cord for real strength — pitch alone is brittle.

Birch bark tar is better still and needs no collecting of resin: heat rolled birch bark in a sealed tin with a small hole, with the hole over a buried container, and a dark tar drips out. Waterproof, strong and slightly flexible.

Casein glue (milk glue) — water resistant

  1. Warm skimmed milk and add vinegar or lemon juice until it curdles.
  2. Strain the curds and rinse well with water, then squeeze dry. (Fat ruins it — skimmed milk only.)
  3. Mash the curds with a little slaked lime (wood ash lye, or lime from burnt limestone or shells) until it turns into a smooth, honey-like liquid.
  4. Use within a few hours — it hardens in the pot.

Stronger than hide glue once set and far more water resistant, though not waterproof. This is what early aircraft and laminated beams were glued with.

Starch paste (paper, cloth, cordage, light work)

Mix flour or starch with cold water to a cream, then stir into boiling water and cook until it thickens and turns translucent. A pinch of salt or a few drops of pine oil slows mould. Good for paper, cardboard, labels, papier-mâché splints, and sizing cordage. Weak and not water resistant.

Rubber cement and modern scavenging

  • Tyre inner tubes dissolved in petrol or acetone make a rubber cement for patching rubber and sealing seams. Vapour is explosive — outdoors only, no flames.
  • Polystyrene foam dissolved in acetone or petrol makes a strong, waterproof, gap-filling glue and sealant. Same warning.
  • Melted plastic (HDPE from milk jugs, PP from containers) works as hot-melt glue and filler. Melt at low heat in a tin — never burn plastic, and keep the air moving.

Joints that hold without glue

Glue is a bonus. These carry the load on their own:

  • Mortise and tenon, pegged: a square hole and a matching tongue, locked with a tapered hardwood peg (trenail). Drill the peg hole in the tenon slightly offset toward the shoulder so driving the peg pulls the joint tight ("drawboring"). This is the strongest joint you can make with hand tools, and it survives wet and dry cycles that would destroy glue.
  • Lap and half-lap: two pieces each cut half-depth so they overlap flush. Peg or lash.
  • Dovetail: flared tails that cannot pull apart in one direction. Boxes, drawers, chest corners.
  • Scarf joint: long tapered overlap for joining two pieces end to end. Make the taper at least 8× the thickness, and peg or lash it.
  • Wedged tenon: pass the tenon right through and drive a wedge into a saw cut in its end. Tightens permanently. Used on tool handles, stools and benches.
  • Lashing: cord wrapped square, diagonal or shear, with frapping turns pulled tight between the poles (see Essential Knots). Lashed frames flex instead of breaking, and can be taken apart and rebuilt. Wet rawhide lashing shrinks as it dries and grips like iron.

Pressure and clamping with nothing

  • Rope tourniquet (Spanish windlass): loop cord around the work, put a stick through the loop and twist.
  • Wedges driven between the work and a fixed block.
  • Weight: stones, water containers.
  • Green-wood spring clamps: a split length of springy wood.
  • Rubber strips cut from inner tubes, wrapped tight.

Bicycle Repair & Maintenance

Transport

A bicycle carries a person 5× further than walking for the same energy, and can carry 30–100 kg of cargo with a trailer. No fuel. Keep bikes running.

Fixing a flat tyre

  1. Open the brake if it blocks the wheel (rim brakes: squeeze the caliper and lift the cable end). Remove the wheel (quick-release lever or 15 mm nuts).
  2. Let all air out. Push the tyre beads into the centre of the rim all the way round — this gives slack.
  3. Opposite the valve, insert a tyre lever (the printed Tyre Levers) under the bead, lever it over the rim and hook the lever to a spoke. Insert a second lever 10 cm along and slide it around the rim.
  4. Pull out the tube. Find the cause: run fingers carefully inside the tyre for thorns, glass, wire. Remove it, or the new tube will puncture too.
  5. Find the hole: pump air in, listen, or hold under water and look for bubbles.
  6. Patch: roughen around the hole with sandpaper, spread a thin layer of rubber cement, wait until it's dry to the touch (3–5 min), press the patch on firmly for a minute.
  7. Put a little air in the tube to give it shape. Valve through the rim hole, tube inside the tyre. Push the bead back on with your thumbs, finishing at the valve. Avoid levers when refitting — they pinch the tube.
  8. Inflate partway, check the tube isn't caught under the bead all round, then inflate to the pressure on the tyre sidewall.

No patch kit? Tie a knot in the tube at the hole (shortens it, but works). Or stuff the tyre tightly with grass, leaves or rags to ride slowly home. A strip of old tyre or a banknote inside the tyre covers a cut in the tyre wall ("tyre boot").

Puncture-proofing: an old tube with the valve cut out, slit and laid inside the tyre as a liner. Or a solid "tyre" of garden hose for very slow cargo use.

Chain

  • Clean and lube every few hundred km and after rain: wipe, apply a drop of oil on each link, wipe the excess off. Motor oil, chainsaw bar oil, or vegetable oil in an emergency.
  • Broken chain: with a chain tool, push out a pin from a damaged link, remove it and rejoin (the chain is shorter — avoid the largest gears). A "quick link" makes it tool-free.
  • Worn chain stretches and wears the gears. If you can lift the chain off the front of the big chainring by more than a few mm, it's worn.

Brakes

  • Rim brakes: pads should hit the rim squarely, not the tyre. Replace when grooves wear away. Adjust cable tension with the barrel adjuster at the lever.
  • Wet rims stop poorly — brake early, pump the brakes to clear water.
  • Disc brakes: keep oil off rotors and pads. Contaminated pads can be sanded or baked on a stove burner briefly to burn off oil.

Gears (derailleur)

  • Chain is slow to move onto a larger rear cog (easier gear), or rattles → turn the barrel adjuster (where the cable enters the derailleur or shifter) half a turn anticlockwise to add cable tension.
  • Slow to move onto a smaller cog (harder gear) → turn it clockwise.
  • Bent derailleur hanger (after a fall) → gently straighten by eye so the derailleur hangs vertically below the cogs.
  • If gears fail entirely: shorten the chain and set it on a single middle gear ("singlespeed") to get home.

Spokes and wheels

A wobbly wheel usually means loose or broken spokes. Tighten loose spokes on the side the rim pulls away from, a quarter-turn at a time. Ride carefully with a broken spoke — open the brake if the wheel rubs.

Carrying loads

  • Rear rack + panniers (the printed Bucket Pannier Hooks turn plastic buckets into waterproof panniers).
  • Trailer (printed Trailer Hitch for light loads — ride slowly).
  • Keep heavy items low and centred; balance left and right.
  • Walk the bike uphill with heavy loads; on steep descents with cargo, brake early and gently with both brakes.

Essential spares

Tubes, patch kit, tyre levers, pump, chain tool, quick links, chain oil, brake pads, a brake cable and gear cable, Allen keys (4, 5, 6 mm), 15 mm wrench, spoke key, zip ties, duct tape.

Carts, Trailers & Carrying Loads

Transport

Moving water, firewood, food, the injured and belongings is daily work after a disaster. Wheels and simple machines multiply what people can do.

Carrying on foot

  • Backpack: heavy items close to your spine and between the shoulder blades. Hip belt takes the weight.
  • Tumpline: a strap across the forehead (or upper chest) to a load on the back — used worldwide for very heavy loads.
  • Yoke / shoulder pole: two buckets balanced from a pole across the shoulders. Walk with a short, springy step.
  • Water: 20 litres = 20 kg. Two 10-litre containers balance better than one 20-litre.
  • Travois: two long poles crossed and tied at the top, the bottom ends dragging, a platform lashed between. Drag loads (or an injured person) over rough ground where wheels fail.

Building a hand cart

Parts

  • 4 × printed Cart Wheels (180 mm), 8 × 608 bearings (skateboards, inline skates, scooters).
  • 2 × M8 threaded rods or long bolts as axles, nuts, and printed Axle Spacers and washers.
  • Printed Tube Frame Connectors (corner3, tee, side-tee) and PVC pipe, electrical conduit, bamboo or straight poles.
  • Plywood, planks, or a lashed deck of sticks.

Steps

  1. Frame: a rectangle (e.g. 100 × 60 cm) using four corner3 connectors at the corners — the vertical sockets point up to make side rails. Add a cross-member with tee connectors in the middle.
  2. Axle mounts: drill 8.5 mm holes through blocks of hardwood bolted under the frame, or through the frame pipes themselves (reinforce PVC with a wooden dowel inside).
  3. Wheels: bolt each wheel with a spacer between the wheel's bearing and the frame, washer and two nuts locked against each other on the outside.
  4. Handle: a long pipe from a tee at the front, with a cross-handle at hand height.
  5. Deck: lash or screw on planks.

Two-wheel carts (one axle under the middle of the load) are easier to pull over rough ground; four-wheel carts carry heavier loads on smooth roads. Keep the load centred over (or slightly in front of) a single axle.

Load limits

Each printed wheel ~25 kg. Four wheels → ~100 kg on smooth ground. Use bigger wooden or recycled bicycle wheels for heavier loads — bicycle wheels on a pipe frame make excellent carts.

Bicycle trailer

Same frame, two wheels, a tongue (single pole) running forward to the bike, ending in a wooden fork. The printed Trailer Hitch clamps on the seat post; an 8 mm bolt through the hitch and fork lets it swivel. Wrap rubber around the joint so it can also tilt. Light loads, slow speeds.

Simple machines

Lever

Moving a load 10× heavier than you can lift needs a lever 10× longer on your side of the pivot than on the load side. A long pole and a rock fulcrum moves boulders, lifts vehicles off people, pulls stakes.

Kites, Ziplines & Gliding Devices

Transport

Air and gravity will move things for you if you let them. The useful versions — kites, ziplines, ropeways, dropped cargo gliders — need no engine and no fuel. The version everyone asks about first, a home-made glider carrying a person, is covered at the end, honestly.

Why bother

After a disaster the obstacles are gaps: a flooded river, a collapsed bridge, a ravine, a gorge, a line of rooftops, a valley between two settlements. Carrying a load the long way round can cost a day. A line across the gap costs an hour and then serves forever.

1. Kites

The most useful flying thing you can build, and the one with real jobs to do.

Jobs a kite does

  • Signalling. A kite flies hundreds of metres up and is visible for kilometres — far better than a ground panel. Fly bright cloth, foil strips or an emergency blanket. At night, a small light or chemical light stick on the line. See Signalling for Rescue.
  • Lofting a radio antenna. This is the big one. A wire hoisted 30–100 m by a kite turns a weak HF radio into one that reaches across a country. See Radio Communications. Never fly near power lines, and bring it down before any sign of a thunderstorm — a wet line and a wire antenna in a storm is lethal.
  • Getting a line across a gap. Fly a light kite with a thin line over a river or ravine in a crosswind; someone on the far side catches it. Use the thin line to pull a stronger one, and the strong one to pull a rope. This is how the first ropes for real bridges have been placed for centuries.
  • Pulling. A big kite pulls hard. Kite-assisted carts, sleds and boats work with steady wind — a genuine way to move loads with no fuel.
  • Lifting a camera or mirror to see over terrain.

A simple kite that works

A sled kite or barn-door/diamond kite is forgiving and easy to build.

Diamond kite:

  1. Two sticks (light, straight — bamboo, split cane, thin dowels): one vertical about 90 cm, one horizontal about 75 cm.
  2. Lash the horizontal across the vertical about 1/4 down from the top.
  3. Run a string around the four tips to make the outline, notching the stick ends to hold it.
  4. Cover with a plastic bag, tarp offcut, paper or light cloth, folded over the outline string and taped or glued.
  5. Bridle: a string from the top of the spine to the bottom, attached to the flying line about 1/3 down. Adjust this until it flies steadily.
  6. Tail: a long strip of cloth or plastic. Too much wobble = add tail. It flies straight up and won't climb = shorten it.

Fly it with your back to the wind, in open ground, away from trees, roads and all power lines.

2. Ziplines and aerial ropeways (the practical "gliding")

A rope between two points at different heights. Loads ride down it under gravity. This is the genuinely valuable technique in this article.

Cargo zipline

  • Anchors: live trees, rock, or deeply buried logs. Wrap wide webbing or doubled rope, never a sharp bend over an edge. Pad anywhere the line crosses rock.
  • Rope: as strong as you can get. Static (low-stretch) rope, steel cable or chain for anything heavy.
  • Slope: about 1 in 10 (a 3 m drop over 30 m) moves cargo without arriving at dangerous speed. Steeper is faster and more violent.
  • Tension: the tighter the line, the enormously higher the forces on the anchors. A tight line can pull with many times the weight of the load. Leave real sag — it is safer and gentler on everything.
  • Trolley: the printed Pulley Sheave and Block Plates running on the rope, or a simple carved hardwood block with a groove, or a loop of rope or a carabiner (fast and rope-burning — use for cheap cargo only).
  • Brake: a bundle of cloth, old tyre or a sandbag at the low end, plus a tail rope held by someone at the bottom. Never let anyone stand in the landing zone.
  • Return line: tie a light line to the trolley so the far side can haul it back. Now it's a two-way system.

Two-way ropeway (a loop)

Rig a continuous loop of rope around a pulley at each end — the printed sheaves work well. Pull one side and the load goes across; the loop brings the carrier back. Used worldwide for crossing gorges, hauling firewood down hillsides, and moving water containers uphill. With a printed Hand Winch at one end, one person can haul loads up a slope that would take four to carry.

Carrying people — a different standard

A cargo zipline is a convenience. A people-carrying one is a life-safety system, and the rules are not the same:

  • Only with proper climbing or rigging rope and hardware, rated and undamaged, with real anchors and a backup anchor
  • Never with printed plastic parts, improvised hooks, or rope of unknown history
  • Never without a separate backup attachment to the line
  • Know that a line failure, a slipped anchor, or arriving at speed all cause serious or fatal injury

If someone needs to cross and there is any other way — a longer walk, waiting for water to drop, a proper boat — take it.

3. Cargo gliders and parachutes (unmanned)

Dropping supplies to people you cannot reach: across a flooded area, down a cliff, to an isolated house.

  • Cargo parachute: a square of cloth, tarp or plastic sheet with lines from each corner to the load. Rough guide: about 1 m² of canopy per 1–2 kg for a gentle landing. Cut a small hole in the centre — it stops the canopy rocking and spilling air. Pack food, not eggs.
  • Simple cargo glider: a flat or slightly curved wing with the load hung below and slightly forward of the wing's centre, and a tail surface behind. Trim it with test throws: diving means the load is too far forward, stalling and tumbling means too far back. It will fly perhaps 5–8 metres forward for every metre it drops. Useful for sending a line, a note, or a small package across a gap or down a hillside.
  • Message delivery: a weighted paper or cloth streamer is more reliable than a glider in any wind. For a line across a gap, a weight on a line thrown by hand or a simple sling beats both.

4. Human-carrying gliders — read this before you build one

People do build hang gliders, paragliders and ultralights, and in normal times they do it with engineered designs, certified materials, instruction, and a progression that starts with running down a gentle slope a metre off the ground.

Building one from tarps, bamboo, scrap tube and improvised fittings, in a crisis, and launching off high ground is one of the most reliably fatal things in this entire library. This isn't caution for its own sake — the specific reasons are:

  • Loads are much higher than intuition says. A wing pulling 2–3 G in a gust puts several times the pilot's weight through the spars and fittings. Improvised joints, unknown tube, and lashings fail — and a wing failure in flight is unsurvivable.
  • You cannot test it safely. Every part of the envelope has to be explored in the air, and the first discovery of a flaw happens at height.
  • Stability and control are not obvious. Centre of gravity, washout, dihedral and sweep decide whether a wing recovers from a gust or tumbles. Getting these wrong produces something that flies fine for ten seconds and then departs.
  • Landing is the hard part. Most injuries in real hang gliding happen at launch and landing, to trained pilots with proper gear.
  • A broken back in a place with no hospital is a death sentence, and it costs your group its ability to feed and shelter everyone, because now they are carrying you.

The honest calculation: a glider descends. It gets you down a hill and over an obstacle once, then it's luggage. The same effort spent on a zipline, a raft, a cart, a repaired bicycle, or simply walking a longer route gets you there repeatedly, with both legs.

If you genuinely must cross a gap, the solutions in this library that actually work are: a kite-placed line, then a rope, then a zipline or ropeway (above); a raft or boat; a longer route on foot or by bike. All of them are survivable when they go wrong.

If you want to fly, learn it properly from instructors with proper equipment, now, while that's possible. Then you'll have both the skill and the judgement — and judgement is the part that keeps people alive.

Finding Water

Water

How much

  • Drinking: 2–3 litres per person per day at rest in mild weather; 4–6+ litres in heat or hard work.
  • Total with cooking and basic hygiene: about 4 litres (1 gallon) per person per day as an absolute minimum; 15 litres is the humanitarian standard.
  • Signs of dehydration: dark urine, headache, dizziness, little urine. Aim for pale-yellow urine.
  • Ration sweat, not water. Work in the cool of the day, rest in shade, keep clothes on in the sun, don't eat much if you have very little water (digestion uses water).

In and around buildings

  • Water heater tank (turn off power/gas first, open the drain valve at the bottom — sediment first, then clean water).
  • Toilet cisterns (the tank, not the bowl, and not if chemical cleaners were added).
  • Pipes: open the highest tap in the building to let air in, then drain from the lowest tap.
  • Swimming pools and hot tubs — usable for washing and flushing; for drinking only after filtering and boiling (pool chemicals may be concentrated).
  • Canned fruit and vegetable liquids.

Outdoors

  • Rain: the best source. Catch it from roofs (discard the first few minutes' flow, which washes off dust and droppings), tarps, plastic sheets. See Rainwater & Storage.
  • Springs and streams: choose flowing water, upstream of settlements, farms and roads. Still treat it.
  • Valleys and low ground: water collects at the bottom. Look for green vegetation, reeds, willows, cottonwoods, ferns.
  • Animal signs: insects (bees fly in straight lines to water), birds circling low at dawn and dusk, converging game trails.
  • Dry riverbeds: dig at the outside of a bend, at the lowest point. If the sand is damp, dig down and wait for water to seep in.
  • Dew: wipe grass and plants with a cloth at dawn and wring it out. Tie cloths around your ankles and walk through wet grass.
  • Snow and ice: melt it before drinking — eating snow lowers body temperature. Ice gives more water per fuel than snow. Melt a little water first in the pot, then add snow, so the pot doesn't scorch.

Solar still and transpiration bags

  • Transpiration bag: tie a clear plastic bag over a leafy (non-toxic) branch in sun; water collects in the low corner. 0.2–1 litre/day per bag.
  • Solar still (hole in the ground with plastic sheet): usually produces very little — often less than you sweat digging it. Use only to distil salty or urine-contaminated water.

Avoid

  • Water with dead animals nearby, oily sheen, foam, unusual colours or algae blooms (blue-green scum can be toxic even after boiling).
  • Runoff from mines, farms (fertiliser, pesticides), roads, and industrial areas.

Making Water Safe

Water

Untreated water from rivers, lakes, rain barrels, floodwater and damaged mains can carry bacteria, viruses and parasites that cause diarrhoea — which kills through dehydration. Treat all water unless it comes from a sealed bottle.

Clear the water first, then disinfect it. Filters, cloth and settling do not make water safe on their own.

Step 1 — Clear it

  1. Let cloudy water stand for a few hours so dirt settles. Pour off the top.
  2. Pour through a clean cloth folded several times (or a coffee filter).
  3. For very muddy water, a sand/charcoal filter helps (see the Bucket Gravity Filter Cartridge in 3D Prints).

Step 2 — Disinfect it (choose one)

Boiling — most reliable

  • Bring to a rolling boil for 1 minute.
  • Above 2,000 m (6,500 ft) altitude: boil 3 minutes.
  • Let it cool covered. To improve flat taste, pour between two clean containers.

Boiling kills bacteria, viruses and parasites. It does not remove chemicals, fuel or heavy metals.

Household bleach (unscented, plain chlorine bleach)

Use bleach with 5–9% sodium hypochlorite. No scented, "splash-less", or colour-safe types.

Water purification tablets

Follow the packet exactly. Chlorine dioxide tablets work against more organisms than chlorine; most need 30 minutes to 4 hours.

Solar disinfection (SODIS) — no fuel needed

  • Clear PET plastic bottles, 2 litres or smaller, unscratched, labels removed.
  • Water must be fairly clear (you can read large print through the bottle).
  • Fill, shake for 20 seconds (oxygen helps), fill to the top and cap.
  • Lay bottles on their side in full sun for 6 hours, or 2 full days if more than half cloudy. A dark metal roof sheet behind them helps.
  • Does not work in continuous rain. Do not use glass (blocks UV) or PVC.

Filters

Commercial hollow-fibre filters (0.1–0.2 micron) remove bacteria and parasites but not viruses. In areas with sewage contamination (floods, cities), filter and chlorinate or boil.

Storing treated water

  • Clean, covered containers; pour or use a tap — don't dip cups or hands in.
  • Clean containers with 1 teaspoon of bleach per litre of water, shake, wait 30 seconds, rinse with treated water.
  • Re-treat stored water every 6 months, or if you doubt it.

Never drink

  • Seawater or urine — they speed up dehydration.
  • Water with a fuel or chemical smell, or near mines, factories, or floodwater from industrial areas — boiling concentrates chemicals. Find another source.
  • Water from car radiators (antifreeze is poisonous).

Rainwater Catchment & Storage

Water

How much a roof catches

Litres = roof area (m²) × rainfall (mm) × 0.8

A 50 m² roof in 10 mm of rain yields about 400 litres. That's a week of drinking water for 15 people from one light rain. (In US units: 1 inch of rain on 1,000 sq ft ≈ 600 gallons.)

Simple system

  1. Gutters (or a tarp or plastic sheet sloped to a corner).
  2. First-flush diverter: the first water off a roof carries droppings and dust. Let the first 1–2 litres per 10 m² of roof run to waste, or into a separate pipe section with a slow-drain hole at its bottom. The printed Hose Barb tee helps here.
  3. Screen over the tank inlet to keep out leaves, mosquitoes and animals.
  4. Covered tank — opaque so algae can't grow.
  5. Tap above the bottom so sediment stays behind.
  6. Overflow pipe pointing away from the house foundation.

Treat rainwater before drinking (see Making Water Safe), especially from old roofs, lead flashing or tar.

Storage containers

  • Use food-grade plastic (HDPE, marked ♲2, or PETE ♲1), stainless steel, or glass.
  • Never use containers that held chemicals, fuel or pesticides.
  • Keep out of sunlight, away from fuel and chemicals, off concrete floors (chemicals can leach in hot weather).
  • Label with date. Re-treat every 6 months.
  • Smaller containers (10–20 L) are easier to carry, clean and share; 20 L weighs 20 kg.

Gravity distribution

Water flows downhill without pumps. A tank raised 1 m gives a gentle pressure for washing and drip irrigation. Every 10 m of height gives about 1 bar (14.5 psi). A siphon (hose filled with water, one end in the tank, the other lower) moves water over a wall or out of a barrel.

Mosquitoes

Any open standing water breeds mosquitoes within a week. Cover everything, tip out puddles and containers, and put a few drops of cooking oil on water that is not for drinking.

Water From Air - Dew, Fog & Condensation

Water

Air always holds water. Getting it out needs either a surface colder than the dew point (condensation) or fog droplets physically intercepted by a mesh. Both work, but yields vary enormously with climate — know which methods suit yours before you need them.

Reality check: these methods supplement a water supply. Except for fog nets in the right terrain, none of them will keep a person alive on their own. A person needs 2–3 litres a day minimum. Always run these alongside rain catchment and any surface water. See Finding Water and Rainwater Catchment & Storage.

The one rule that governs all of it

Water condenses when a surface is colder than the dew point of the surrounding air. That's why dew forms on clear, still nights — surfaces radiate heat to the open sky and drop below air temperature. This means:

  • Clear nights with no wind and high humidity = good. Cloudy, windy nights = little or nothing.
  • The surface must see open sky, not be under trees or a roof.
  • Thin, lightweight materials that cool fast work better than thick ones that hold heat.
  • Humid coastal and desert-coastal climates give the best yields. Hot dry inland air with low humidity gives almost nothing.

1. Dew collection (best yield-to-effort of the condensation methods)

Typical yield: 0.1–0.3 litres per square metre per night in favourable conditions. A 10 m² sheet might give 1–3 litres.

Dew sheet:

  1. Lay a plastic sheet, tarp or metal panel on open ground, sloped to a low corner, clear of trees.
  2. Weight the edges. Dig a small pit at the low corner with a container in it.
  3. Collect before sunrise — dew evaporates within minutes of the sun hitting it.

Dew from vegetation (no materials needed): before dawn, drag a clean absorbent cloth or tied bundles of grass through dew-wet grass and low plants, and wring it into a container. Tying cloths around your ankles and walking through a wet meadow is a classic method and genuinely works. Avoid areas with animal droppings, roadsides and sprayed fields, and treat the water (see Making Water Safe).

Improve any dew surface: metal or glass cools faster than plastic. Raising the sheet slightly off the ground on stones lets air reach both sides and increases yield.

2. Fog nets (by far the highest yield — where fog exists)

In fog-belt terrain this is a serious water supply, not a trick. Working installations in coastal Chile, Peru, Morocco and Nepal collect 2–10 litres per square metre per day, and some sites far more. A 40 m² net can supply a household.

Where it works: coastal hills and ridges with regular sea fog, mountain cloud belts, and high ground where cloud blows through. Where it doesn't: anywhere fog is rare. Walk your terrain at dawn and see.

How to build one:

  1. Mesh: shade cloth, mosquito netting, fine plastic mesh, or doubled polypropylene netting. Roughly 35–50% of the area should be solid — too fine and the fog flows around it instead of through, too open and droplets pass straight through.
  2. Frame: two stout poles with the mesh stretched between them like a sail, facing into the prevailing fog wind. Guy the poles properly — a wet net in wind pulls hard.
  3. Gutter along the bottom edge: a split pipe, a length of bamboo split lengthways, or a folded plastic sheet, draining to a covered container.
  4. Put it on an exposed ridge or slope that catches the moving fog, not in a sheltered hollow.

Droplets strike the mesh, merge, run down and drip into the gutter. It needs no power and runs itself. Keep the mesh and gutter clean, and screen the tank against insects.

3. Transpiration bag (small but reliable, almost free)

A clear plastic bag tied over a leafy branch. The plant pulls groundwater up and transpires it; it condenses inside the bag.

  • Yield: 0.1–1 litre per bag per day. Set up ten bags, not one.
  • Use non-toxic trees — avoid oleander, yew, rhododendron, laurel, poison ivy/oak/sumac and anything with milky sap. If unsure, see Foraging Safely.
  • Choose a leafy branch in full sun. Bigger bag, more leaves, more water.
  • Put a small clean stone in the bag's lowest corner so water pools there, and tie the mouth tightly.
  • Collect in the evening, or fit a tube or a pinched-off corner to tap it without untying.
  • Rotate between branches — a bagged branch suffers if left for days.

Water may taste of the plant. It's generally safe from a known-safe species, but treat it if you can.

4. Vegetation bag (lower yield, no live tree needed)

Fill a clear bag with cut non-toxic green vegetation, seal it, lay it in the sun sloped so condensate runs to a corner. Replace the vegetation every couple of days. Yields less than a transpiration bag, and the water tastes stronger.

5. Solar still — know its limits

A pit lined with plastic over a container, with a weight in the centre to make a cone.

Honestly: a solar still typically produces 0.1–0.5 litres a day and you may sweat more than that digging it in heat. It is not a survival water source. It has two genuinely valuable uses:

  • Distilling undrinkable water: salt water, brackish water, contaminated or chemically tainted water, urine, radiator water. Pour it into the pit around the container (not in it) and collect the pure distillate. This is the only method here that removes salt and most chemicals.
  • Passive background collection when you have spare plastic and nothing else to do with the hole.

The same trick works above ground: a container of foul water in a sealed clear bag or under a sloped sheet, in the sun, with a clean collector where the condensate runs down.

6. Condensing from cold surfaces

If you have anything cold — a cave wall, a cold spring, metal that was outside overnight, a container of cool water from a well — warm humid air blown or fanned across it will condense water on it. In humid climates, wiping down cold metal surfaces (vehicle bodywork, metal roofs, tanks) at dawn with a cloth collects real amounts.

Powered dehumidifiers pull litres per day from humid air, but cost 200–1,000 Wh per day — see Power & Light. That's a big solar array for a modest amount of water. Only worth it where the power is abundant and no other source exists. Clean the coils and tank, and treat the water before drinking.

Safety

Condensed and distilled water is biologically clean when it forms, but it picks up contamination from the sheet, gutter, container and insects immediately after.

  • Keep collection surfaces and containers clean; rinse new plastic sheets.
  • Cover storage against insects and animals.
  • Distilled water has no minerals and tastes flat. That's fine short term; over months, get minerals from food or add a pinch of salt.
  • Still treat it before drinking if you have the means (see Making Water Safe) — the cost is low and the risk of a dirty tarp is real.
